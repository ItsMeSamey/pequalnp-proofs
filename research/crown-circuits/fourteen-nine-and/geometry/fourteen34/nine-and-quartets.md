# At most one branching quartet with nine AND gates

Use the regular size-34, nine-AND host. Its decoder OR budget is q−3. The degree bound is four, and every branching flag is a pure quartet with two OR readers, by degree/t14-34/small-branches.md and the high-degree remote component argument. Every component containing a degree-four vertex has at least two detached components that each contain a pure flag of degree at least three. Indeed, components containing only mixed flags and private pure doubletons can be erased at their full vertex count by contracting mixed injection ORs and their distinct clean terminal collector slots. Then use the center-layer and detached-component lower bound. Each high-pure detached component has at least three edges.

For a degree-four center, discard mixed-only components and erase every remote mixed flag. Their distinct dedicated ORs can be bypassed while preserving paths from pure flags via the nonraw sides. Write k for the number of edge-containing pure components in the resulting remote pure graph, and h for the number of remote isolated pure flags. The isolated-pure improvement to the layer bound requires k+h−[h>0]≥2. Here the center's own component may be peeled by the ordinary layer argument: fixed remote pure flags cannot affect the center's actual star tests.

Two elementary facts about tiny connected simple graphs will be used. In a degree-four component with at most five edges, the nonleaf vertices form a clique: it is a four-edge star with at most one extra edge, extending a leaf or joining two leaves. The same conclusion holds for a component of degree at least three having at most four edges. In a three-edge component bearing a pure flag of degree at least three, the only possible pure flag is the center of a star.

If a single component contains two degree-four vertices, it uses at least seven edges. Among the two high-pure detached components, one therefore has exactly three edges and the other at most four. Additional components can only be single mixed edges. Of the two detached pure graphs one is isolated and the other contributes at most one component. This gives k+h−[h>0]≤1, a contradiction. Thus even two nonbranching degree-four vertices cannot lie in one component.

Now suppose branching quartets lie in distinct components A and B. There must be a third component C with a high pure flag. Let c be the actual number of components, including mixed-only components. At a branching quartet delete that flag and its t pendant neighbors: this removes at least t+2 ORs, as the t distinct dedicated leaf readers cannot directly read the dominating quartet. If its component now has j nontrivial pieces, the remaining flag graph has q−1−t vertices and c−1+j components, with another intact degree-four vertex. The general component bound gives at least (q−1−t)+4−4−(c−1+j) ORs. Comparing to q−3−(t+2) yields c+j≥5.

* If c=3, both A and B must retain at least two edge-containing pieces. Each therefore contains at least six edges: the quartet's four edges and at least one surviving edge in each piece. Together with C this would require at least fifteen edges.
* If c=4, each must retain an edge, so the only partition is 5+5+3+1. The last component is all mixed. Viewed from A, B has at most one remote pure component and C supplies only an isolate, violating the remote pure bound.
* If c≥5 and the extra components besides A,B,C are all mixed, discard them. Then A and B have at most five edges apiece and C at most four. Each of their pure induced graphs has at most one component. Since A and B each contain their pure quartet and C has at least one pure flag, looking from A forces both B and C to have a nontrivial pure component; looking from B likewise forces A to have a nontrivial pure component. This requires at least five edges in A, five in B and four in C, allowing no extras.
* If c≥5 and an extra component contains a pure flag, simplicity and the minimum pure size two force the exact partition 4+4+3+2+1. Its two four-edge stars, three-edge star and two-edge path have only four nonleaf vertices altogether. A pure quartet requires at least five pure flags by the star bound k+2≤p+1, which is impossible.

Thus there is at most one branching quartet, and at most one branching group of any size.
