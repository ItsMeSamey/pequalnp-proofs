# A branching quartet is the only degree-four flag

Start with nine-and-quartets.md. Suppose A contains the branching quartet and another degree-four flag lies in B. If B has no high pure flag, two additional high-pure components C,D are necessary; the partition is exactly 4+4+3+3, and A is a bare star. This contradicts c+j≥5 from nine-and-quartets.md (c=4 components and j=0 nontrivial pieces after deleting A's quartet and pendant leaves). Otherwise let B be high-pure and let C be another high-pure component.

For c=3 the same inequality forces A to retain at least two nontrivial pieces, so A has at least six edges. B and C then have at most five and four respectively, and at least one is a bare high star. All nonleaves in each of these small components form a clique, giving remote pure slack at most one. For c=4, A uses at least five edges. When the extra component is mixed-only, both B and C must contribute nontrivial pure components to avoid the isolate penalty; they need five and four edges, respectively, exceeding fourteen in total. If the extra component has a pure flag, the partition is exactly 5+4+3+2. The final component is a P3 with private clean doubleton. Erasing its two labels saves five physical gates, and the reduced three-component decoder preserves q'−3 and live pure paths exactly as in the 6+3+3+2 case of nine-and-completion.md. B and C now contribute at most one isolated pure flag each, so the pure slack test fails.

For c≥5 use the all-mixed and pure-extra counts in nine-and-quartets.md. The center test at B to get a nontrivial pure component in A requires only that B have a degree-four vertex, not that B branch.
