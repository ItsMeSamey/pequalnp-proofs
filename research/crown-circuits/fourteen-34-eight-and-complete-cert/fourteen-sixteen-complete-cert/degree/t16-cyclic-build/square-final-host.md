# The square residual exceeds twelve ORs and eight ANDs

Use square-regularity.md and clean-terminal normalization. All path flags are single-read, all paths ordinary, and independent s has two OR readers. Every AND-read square vertex has a unique reader and its product is clean. No two square vertices share an AND. Adjacent and opposite raw square ORs are impossible (square-no-nonempty.md and duality/uniform/sixteen-square-adjacent-or.md). Hence b≤1 by sixteen-cycle-readers.md: a group of only branching square flags would allow a forbidden raw-pair OR.

**A missed edge.** If A has clean reader A∧P, P must be true on BC but false on AB: adding A alone to BC gives the positive represented by BC+AB. Likewise P is true on CD and false on DA. A is unavailable separately to P. In the two compared pairs B,D and all outside flags match. An opposite clean AND-read C would therefore conceal both differences since its sole signal is zero on all four singletons. C cannot directly be P since their shared AND was excluded. Thus the clean AND-read square vertices contain no opposite pair, so at most two are present, adjacent if two. At least one square edge e misses them all.

**OR accounting.** Charge one OR for each of the six path leaf injections and each of the three path centers (injection or dedicated clean terminal). Distinct path centers have distinct products by sixteen-center-comparison.md. Charge each of the 4−b nonbranch square vertices similarly, using its OR group or its clean AND terminal. The square products are distinct. No square product equals a charged path-center product, since a vertex visible only through conjunction with an outside center would be unable to distinguish a local square singleton from a positive pair when paths are empty. All charged injection groups are distinct by within-component identities, the excluded square-vertex ORs, and the cross-component sole-flag fusions in sixteen-center-branch.md and sixteen-cycle-readers.md. All are hot.

The pair consisting of s and e is positive but activates none of the charged clean products: ordinary centers are absent and no clean square vertex meets e. Hence an extra terminal survives, and each charged clean terminal costs a distinct collector OR. These are disjoint from the hot injections. Thus 9+4−b ORs are needed, forcing b=1 and equality. Each charged hot group is a single physical OR with its assigned single-read flag as a direct input. There is no other hot OR. The two OR readers of s therefore attach s to two distinct assigned flags. Neither can be an uncharged OR on s and the branching vertex.

**Physical host.** All eleven original raws read two ORs, with nineteen ORs total. Flattening gives v group vertices and a relative decoder with 19−(22−v)=v−3 ORs. The square and three two-edge paths supply distinct vertices for their original flags: the single-read OR flags enter mutually distinct groups, the AND-read bank flags survive separately, and the branching square flag has a shared bank. The sole additional raw edge s has endpoints among those vertices, as established above. Simplicity follows from irredundancy and singleton-versus-pair discrimination.

No vertex can have degree three or more in such a host containing a cycle. For a high-degree nonstar component, peel to its centered star to obtain at least its order minus two ORs. Its bank is nonuniversal so each remaining component adds full order. If instead the high-degree component is a bare star, the component containing the square is a remote nonstar; full-order nonstar transfer over the star yields the same joint order-minus-two bound. Other remotes again add full order. Both arguments are the general graph inequalities in ten-forest.md and contradict v−3.

Consequently s joins two path leaves. On the same path it forms a triangle, which together with the square needs their combined order minus two ORs. On different paths it forms a six-vertex path, which with the square has the same lower bound by the square-plus-P5 result followed by one extra endpoint erasure. Other paths charge full order over either nonuniversal base. Both possibilities exceed v−3, ending the proof.
