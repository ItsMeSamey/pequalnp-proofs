# Pure groups at thirteen inputs, 31 gates and seven ANDs

The regular representative has 24 ORs and 26 raw incidences. Its mixed OR excess is r=p−2 where p is the number of pure groups. Let H count connected components in the pure incidence graph. For a pure group of raw size k and pure degree d, the star bounds give k+max(d,2)≤p+2 and k≤H+2. The two-input minor argument of degree/t13-31/triple-ports.md applies without change: every AND has three exclusive singleton witnesses on either side; doubleton pure groups are impossible, and every unshared pure triple feeds its own clean dedicated AND with a shared partner. The branching triples each have two OR readers. Thus the number of triples is at most the six fanout-surplus units. Let s,b count unshared and branching triples.

The inequalities 13≥k+3(H−1) and k≤H+2 give k≤5. There are at most eight pure groups by incidences. If p=8 then ≤6 triples force at least two quartets, so all 26 incidences are pure. A quartet requires H≥2: its component has at least five vertices and every other component at least four, impossible. If a quintet exists then p≥5 and H≥3. At p=5,6,7 the required number of pure edges is at least 3p+2−13, namely 4,7,10. Three components host at most 3,6,10 edges respectively. Equality at seven requires a five-clique and two isolates, consuming at least 16 distinct raw labels. All pure groups are therefore triples or quartets.

The two unshared groups at the endpoints of an edge are the only activated groups when only that edge is set. If two different edges were internal to the unshared triples, then setting both activates just dedicated triple signals and never a first AND: each dedicated product's other parent is shared and stays false until another active signal is created. Thus at most one such edge exists. When at least two labels occur outside all s unshared triples, another clean terminal is required to accept their pair. In every case the total terminal count is at most r+1=p−1.

The C++20 enumeration in groups.cpp tries all pure degree/raw-degree decorations in nondecreasing order and then all graphs realizing that degree sequence. For each graph it tries all assignments of branching status to triples, the unshared-edge test, terminal capacity, and the star bounds. It allows arbitrary mixed incidences after budget checking. Counts concern this relaxation; distinct decorations can be isomorphic. The complete output is in groups.out (run with arguments 7 and p in 2..7). The ranges of possible triple counts are 3 at p=3, 0..4 at p=4, 1..5 at p=5, 1..6 at p=6 and 5..6 at p=7. All p=3 and p=7 decorations have a branching triple; deleting it yields a 24-gate T2^10 minor with seven AND gates.

Every pure quartet has fanout at most two. Removing its labels charges eight raw incidences plus its own fanout surplus. Fanout three exhausts the allowable ten savings from the 21-gate nine-input bound. Then no one-sided dark AND can occur, and before a first fully dark AND the only exposed nonraw dark signal is the quartet, just as in the branching-triple argument. Ten ORs alone would vanish, yielding a nine-input 21-gate circuit with seven ANDs and fourteen ORs, already excluded by the OR-optimal theorem. Fanout two permits at most one direct AND reader; a single such reader costs one extra one-sided dark simplification, giving a nine-input 21-gate, six-AND minor. Two direct AND readers exceed the budget. Their partners stay nonzero upon restriction since otherwise the corresponding gate's product was redundant.

At least one pure group branches. Otherwise the first AND takes two private pure groups U,V. An overlapping raw can be factored out. If they are disjoint, remove U's k labels. The 2k raw incidences and one-sided AND are charged by the dark-edge formula. The still-live opposite private OR tree becomes dead, giving |V|−1≥2 further savings. These at least 2k+3 losses violate the 24-gate threshold at ten inputs (k=3) or the 21-gate threshold at nine inputs (k=4). In particular if no triple branches then a quartet branches.

There is a stronger star test for each pure U. If d is its pure degree and s₀ counts dedicated triples outside its closed pure neighborhood, then k+max(d,2)+s₀≤p+2. Restrict to U's k labels as in the ordinary star proof; the s₀ terminal ANDs are now zero, saving their distinct slots in the final OR collector while at least one terminal remains needed. The early-injection savings occur in groups with a raw operand; the final collector has none. This strengthens the plain star bound independently of the component-peeling bound.

For a pure-only graph restriction, choose any subset of pure vertices, then retain just raw labels joining two chosen vertices. Expose each nonisolated endpoint as a formal input pin. A negative test is the endpoint pair of any one retained edge, and a positive test is the union of the endpoints of two distinct retained edges. Mixed groups receive no raws, so together they contribute at most r binary ORs. Any dedicated terminal whose pure triple has no retained edge is zero; its slot in the final OR collector saves another OR, with at least one surviving terminal whenever the test includes at least two edges. A necessary condition on the retained graph is therefore realizability on these tests with r minus the number of inactive dedicated terminals ORs and arbitrary ANDs. The solver represents each successive OR's two operands, and the output, as conjunctions of subsets of initial pins and preceding ORs, granting empty conjunctions and unused gates. This representation is complete because AND-only subcircuits flatten into products.

colored.cpp applies the stronger star test; induced.cpp enumerates every vertex subset of every surviving decorated graph and identifies isomorphic tests by permutation of nonisolated vertices. For p from three to seven this gives 26,634 decorated graphs and 140 distinct graph/budget queries before monotonicity reduction.

Using the rigid nine-input size-21 profile (four ANDs and seventeen ORs, established in the sibling nine-five and nine-six folders, with the seven-AND case from the OR-optimal theorem), deletion of any four raws saves at most nine gates. For four labels S there is at most one wholly contained pure group, since any two such groups each have size at least three and intersect in at most one raw. Groupwise contraction thus saves at least eight ORs if no pure group disappears and at least seven otherwise. In the latter case follow any path from the erased nonraw pure signal to the output, which remains nonconstant. Its first nonzero group is necessarily an OR group with a zero nonraw operand on the path and at least one surviving operand. This eliminates one additional OR beyond contractions of the removed raw operands there. Thus at least eight ORs disappear. A size-21 minor could then retain at most sixteen ORs from the original 24, violating the nine-input composition theorem.

Consequently every pure quartet satisfies one-surplus deletion accounting, just as a triple does. Every branching pure group has two OR readers and cannot serve as a shared AND partner. Every unshared pure group requires a shared partner. For a quartet whose partner was unshared, after deleting its four labels the one-sided dark AND already uses the sole permitted surplus. Its partner remains nonzero by irredundancy and could be removed as a newly dead wire, producing a forbidden tenth saving. If P is an unshared OR group with a raw x in U, factor x out of the two private OR groups before deleting anything, saving one gate. Otherwise the root of P is not among the raw contractions. Before the dedicated AND, there is no other exposed nonraw dark source because U is the unique erased pure group and its only use is that AND. Consequently the root of an unshared P remains nonzero and uncontracted until orphaned, and the tenth saving is distinct.
