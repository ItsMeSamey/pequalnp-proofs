# Remaining candidate helper counts in the five-axis clean bank

If no full target exists then quartets prune. At most two targets have width below four by the atom bound, and at least twelve require width four. In a bank with p pair helpers and t triples these are obtainable in at most floor(p²/4)+2t ways, by the triangle-free edge bound and two disjoint width-four targets per triple. For p+t≤7 and 2≤p≤6 the bound is below twelve. With at most one pair, every target of width≥3 lies in at most eight atoms, leaving at least six low targets, impossible. Thus p=7, t=0, and the disjoint-axis graph on seven helpers is extremal K₃,₄. All twelve edge products occur. The remaining two targets both have width three and the cube tiles. Pair patterns from opposite shores use disjoint coordinate sets. On each shore their faces are disjoint because every product with any fixed opposite helper must be disjoint from all other such products. The four patterns fill the whole of their shore; the other three fill three quarters. Thus the twelve products fill the cylinder over the three patterns. Neither remaining target may contain one of the three-side helpers. A four-side helper and one literal cannot restrict the other shore to its missing quarter, and two four-side helpers conflict. Hence a full receiver is required.

At least three triples are needed by at-least-three-triples.md. With at most two pair helpers and at least three triples at most two quartets remain. Any nonquartet full uses both pair helpers: two triples based on the same pair span at most four axes and a complementary pair cannot be its triple's generating pair. Their intersection has at most four atoms, so at most eight fulls exist in all. All width≥3 targets lie in the two helper faces, occupying at most sixteen atoms. At most two low targets (width≤2) fit by cube volume. Thus 2(14−l)−a≤16 forces l=2,a=8 and disjoint helper faces. But disjointness forbids all nonquartet full targets, contradiction. At least three pair helpers are needed.

With three pair helpers, at most four triples and no quartet, count 24 atoms over the three pair faces with multiplicity. Each full is contained in at least two distinct pair faces. A width-four face uses at least one and charges two atoms; a width-three face charges at least four. Thus 24≥2(14−l)+2n₃ for l low targets and n₃ of width three. Equality forces l=2,n₃=0. Both low targets have width two (width one and width two leave only eight atoms for twelve targets). No pair helper meets the two low targets, and every doubly covered atom is full with no triply covered atom, since every width-four target pays two atoms through one entire containing pair and has no further incidence budget.

The low faces must conflict on some axis c. If their other fixed axes a,b differ, precisely three pair patterns avoid both: the sign opposite the first c and opposite the second b; opposite the first a and opposite the second c; or C=(opposite the first a, opposite the second b). These are all the helpers, and their double-covered region is exactly C. With the same two axes, diagonally opposite low faces leave only two eligible patterns. Adjacent low faces together fill one halfcube, forcing each pair helper to contain the opposite sign plus one further literal. No triple coverage requires two of these three literals to be complementary. Again the third helper C is exactly the double-covered region.

In both cases C has eight atoms, split into two panels on another axis, each having the same two free axes d,e and one other pair helper which is absent on the opposite panel. All eight assignments must be full. Neither d nor e is present in any pair helper. To cover the four full assignments on either panel requires four distinct triple helpers, one adding each of d+, d−, e+, e−. With ≤4 available, each must serve both panels, so each can only be based on C. Two triples based on C specify at most four axes, and joining one to a pair cannot supply both d and e. Thus this bank is impossible.

The possible profiles (p,t,u) are consequently (4,3,0) and (3,3,1).
