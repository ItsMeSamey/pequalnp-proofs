# Three-colour decision after sparse cuts and high-degree conditioning

On a simple undirected graph remove vertices of degree at most two until none remain. Every coloring of the resulting 3-core extends in reverse deletion order. Treat connected core components independently. In each, enumerate the proper assignments to its k vertices of core degree at least four. Every remaining vertex v has three colors minus those used by its assigned neighbors. Its list has size at least its residual degree, since v originally had degree three. The degree-choosability theorem guarantees every connected residual component that is not a Gallai tree has a list coloring. A Gallai tree has only clique and odd-cycle blocks. On its block-cut tree, pass allowed cut-vertex colors toward the root. For a clique test all injective assignments (size at most four); for a cycle fix the parent color, propagate the two-neighbor inequality along the path, and test the closing edge. Intersect messages at a vertex and include its own list. This also decides isolated vertices, including empty lists. The whole procedure decides in O*(3^k) for maximum k over components. It gives polynomial time at k=O(log n) and subexponential time at k=o(n). The executable returns only a Boolean. The degree-list theorem is stated in [Cranston and Rabern](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.00350).

For wheels on a rim of length at least four there is exactly one high-degree vertex in the core. After coloring the hub, the rim must alternate the other two colors, so even rims pass and odd rims fail. Both families have unbounded order and degree, contain triangles, and require a linear conditioning parameter on the previously studied direct one-hot counting interface. This illustrates a decision/count distinction. Checker coverage includes all 209 unlabeled graphs on at most six vertices and 22 wheel sizes.

A second simplification applies to any edge cut of size at most two. Independently color the two sides, then permute the color names on one side. A crossing edge excludes exactly two of the six permutations, so a permutation meeting all crossing constraints always exists. Recursively separating on such cuts preserves the conjunction of the side decisions. The bound two is sharp even for a matching cut. Put a triangle on one side, and on the other make three pairwise nonadjacent vertices, each adjacent to both endpoints of a single common edge. The three vertices must share one color, whereas the triangle uses all colors; joining the two triples by a matching makes the graph uncolorable despite both sides being colorable. For supplied side colorings and any cut size, build a 3-by-3 bipartite graph of allowed relabelings by deleting the color pairs that occur on cut edges. A perfect matching is equivalent to gluing those particular side colorings. Failure for one pair of side colorings is insufficient to reject the original graph in general.
