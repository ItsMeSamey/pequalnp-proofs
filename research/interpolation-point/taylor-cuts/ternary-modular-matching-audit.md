# Prescribed modular matching and the two cycle-search obstacles

For a graph with GF(3) edge weights and a base perfect matching M of residue r, seeking residue r+1 is equivalent to finding one M-alternating cycle of gain 1 or two vertex-disjoint M-alternating cycles both of gain 2. Gains count inserted edges minus removed ones. Indeed the symmetric difference with a target matching is a disjoint union of cycles; without a gain-1 cycle its gain-2 cycles must number at least two. Sufficiency follows by flipping. Neither individual cycle length is bounded, as seen already on one arbitrarily long even cycle with only one weighted edge.

Both individual searches are NP-hard even for bipartite matching contractions. Given a directed two-disjoint-paths instance on four distinct terminals, retain its arcs with label 0. For the first search add two marked arcs t1→s2 and t2→s1 with label 2. A simple directed cycle of gain 1 must use both marked arcs, resulting in exactly the prescribed disjoint paths after deleting them. For the second search add t1→s1 and t2→s2 with label 2. Two disjoint gain-2 cycles must each use one of these arcs, again giving the specified paths. Private subdivisions remove any duplicate arcs. Any resulting digraph is the contraction of a bipartite matching: use matched pairs L_v,R_v of weight zero and an edge L_u,R_v of the given weight per directed arc u→v. The union decision in these two reductions is easy by forcing the two marked edges and extending to any perfect matching. Thus neither individual hardness proof implies hardness of prescribed-residue matching. A nonzero walk with prescribed gain is also insufficient: walking twice round the sole gain-2 cycle gives gain 1 but there is no such matching.

Avoidance of a single forbidden residue admits a deterministic algorithm on arbitrary graphs. Subtract the forbidden label at the star of one vertex, so target avoidance becomes a nonzero linear functional on matching vectors. Lovász's deterministic finite-field perfect-matching span algorithm determines whether this functional is nonzero on any matching, and edge deletion self-reduces to an actual witness. The same proof tests escape from one affine subspace in GF(3)^b by passing to the quotient. The empty graph is handled directly. [El Maalouly, Steiner and Wulf, Theorem 20](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2023.28). An alternative polynomial algorithm returns an integral lattice basis consisting of actual matchings on matching-covered graphs. [A Polyhedral Perspective on the Perfect Matching Lattice](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03863).

The prescribed singleton residue excludes two residues in GF(3). A primary account distinguishes the polynomial parity algorithm and other open congruency constraints. Randomized strongly polynomial congruency algorithms already include modulus three for bipartite matching as a totally unimodular problem. [STACS 2024](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.STACS.2024.33) · [ICALP 2024](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2024.86).

For a bipartite contraction, any nonzero gain cycle is easy directly: in its group-state lift look for a path from (v,0) to (v,h≠0); decompose the projected closed walk and keep a nonzero simple cycle. If two different strongly connected components each admit such a cycle, every target residue is reachable by flipping a subset of the two cycles. This localizes the difficult case for a non-base target to exactly one active component.
