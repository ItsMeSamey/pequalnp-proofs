# Marked induced-degree parities with syndrome constraints on circle graphs

On a circle graph G with a_i in GF(2), l_i in GF(2)^h and a specified t, one can find a vertex set S satisfying deg_{G[S]}(i)=a_i (mod 2) for every selected i and sum_{i in S} l_i=t, or report failure, with one-sided error bounded by any epsilon. The running time is 2^h poly(n,log(1/epsilon)), with a verified witness on every successful run and a tunable probability of a false negative on feasible inputs. The proof gives a deterministic linear-size reduction to syndrome-constrained perfect matching and then a randomized polynomial identity test. A double-occurrence chord word can be supplied or recovered by polynomial-time circle-graph recognition. [Gioan, Paul, Tedder and Corneil](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3284)

## Binary assignments to the arcs of the chord word

Choose a cyclic orientation and start point of a chord word. Its 2n endpoints are visits, two per chord. Between consecutive visits place a binary variable f_e on their intervening arc, including the wraparound arc. For each chord i consider both visits in their circular linearization order. Write d_j=f_out−f_in as an ordinary integer for its j-th visit. Impose the integer equality

    d_1 + (−1)^(a_i) d_2 = 0.

From a set S, choose the initial arc bit arbitrarily and flip it on passing each occurrence of a selected chord. This is consistent around the circle. An unselected chord has both differences zero. If i is selected, let k be the number of selected endpoints strictly between its two occurrences. Modulo two, k is exactly the induced degree of i, because another chord contributes one iff it crosses i and otherwise contributes zero or two. When k is even, the second flip has the opposite sign from the first; when k is odd, both flips have the same sign. These are precisely the two cases of the displayed equality.

Conversely, any binary arc assignment obeying the equalities has either two zero differences at a chord or two nonzero differences. Read S from the latter case; the same interval argument proves the degree requirements. Each S has exactly the two complementary arc assignments. Its syndrome is linear modulo two in arc bits, since membership of i is f_in1 XOR f_out1. Collecting these coefficients gives arc labels k_e with sum_e k_e f_e=t.

## An ordinary perfect-matching graph

Each chord now has four distinct incidence tokens, one for each of its incoming and outgoing arc ends at both visits. Each token has a signed coefficient +1 or −1 in the integer equality. Exactly two coefficients at a chord are positive and two negative. Normalize its bit to y=f_e at a positive incidence and y=1−f_e at a negative incidence. The chord equality holds iff exactly two of the four y bits are one.

Give each chord two fresh anchors, each adjacent to all four of its tokens. A token matched to an anchor represents y=1. For each arc join its two tokens with one edge if their incidence signs agree. Then precisely both or neither are matched to anchors. If their signs disagree, instead add one fresh vertex adjacent to its two tokens. Exactly one of the two tokens is then matched to an anchor. These conditions identify one consistent f_e on the two ends of each arc. Conversely any allowed arc assignment produces a perfect matching by matching the two local one-tokens to their two anchors in either order and completing each arc gadget. This description also covers consecutive occurrences of one chord and arcs returning to the same chord, since the incidence tokens are distinct.

To encode arc label k_e pick either one of its incidence tokens. Place k_e on both edges joining this token to its local anchors; all other edges initially have zero label. This contributes k_e y in each matching. For each chosen negative incidence add k_e to a fixed offset, since f_e=1+y modulo two there. The matching target is the original t plus the offset. Its matching bits reconstruct the arc assignment and the original S. In particular two assignments and the local anchor permutations do not interfere with the existence reduction.

## Syndrome filtering and witness recovery

On a matching graph with N vertices and labelled edges give each edge a distinct indeterminate X_e and choose a fixed vertex order. For each u in GF(2)^h put X_e times (−1)^(u dot l_e) in the upper-triangle entry of e in a skew-symmetric matrix, and take its Pfaffian; denote the polynomial by P_u. Over any odd field the character average 2^(−h) sum_u (−1)^(u dot t') P_u is the signed generating polynomial of perfect matchings of target t'. Each matching contributes a distinct monomial with coefficient +1 or −1. Therefore it is nonzero exactly on feasible instances. The polynomial degree is at most N/2. This uses only the defining matching expansion of a Pfaffian. Numerical Pfaffians can be evaluated over an odd field by skew Gaussian elimination. [Pfaffian matching expansion](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~jyc/papers/matchgate-paper.pdf)

Evaluate all characters at the same independent random field values for the edge variables. An infeasible instance always evaluates to zero. A feasible one evaluates to zero with probability at most (N/2)/|field| by Schwartz–Zippel. For example, use a polynomial-size odd prime field with size at least 2N and independent repeats for amplification. Compute a witness by testing whether each edge can be deleted and deleting it only on a nonzero evaluation. With simultaneous success of all feasibility tests, the final inclusion-minimal graph admitting the target consists of a perfect matching of that target. Test the final set, map it back and verify it. At most the initial edge count plus one independent oracle calls are needed, so a union bound after logarithmically many repetitions gives the stated error guarantee. An odd number of matching vertices is immediately infeasible. If a Pfaffian orientation of the constructed graph is supplied, one may instead put all variables equal to one and average over the integers using its common-sign matching expansion; there is no cancellation among matchings of a fixed target. This special deterministic assertion requires that orientation.

## Perturbations and deleted vertices

If the adjacency matrix is supplied as G=C+UV^T over GF(2), with C a circle-graph adjacency and k columns in each factor, enumerate z=V^T 1_S. In that branch the C-marks are a+Uz; append the k syndrome rows pinning z. The preceding solver gives 2^(h+2k) poly(n,log(1/epsilon)) time after allocating the error budget across branches. The graph matrices are simple and the displayed equality includes the diagonal. This statement uses a supplied factorization.

A recognizable case is deletion distance at most k to a circle graph. At fixed k enumerate a size-at-most-k modulator D and recognize the remaining graph. Given D, enumerate T=S∩D. For vertices outside D subtract the parity of neighbors in T from their target marks. For each selected vertex i of T append one parity row on its neighbors outside D, with its required RHS adjusted for its neighbors in T. Subtract the syndrome of T from the target. These are all the necessary and sufficient conditions for a solution on the circle graph G−D. The solver uses at most 2^(h+2k) poly(n,log(1/epsilon)) work for a supplied modulator, in addition to n^O(k) recognition if one is not given. No general graph or unrestricted rank-four feasibility algorithm follows.
