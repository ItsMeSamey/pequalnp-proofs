# Finding a nontrivial tight shore

The splitter assumes a connected loopless matching-covered multigraph. Every edge therefore occurs in some perfect matching. Parallel edges can be identified during the search for a vertex shore. Replacing a matched edge by any parallel copy preserves the endpoints and the cut count. The caller retains the original indexed copies when contracting. Both shores of a nontrivial tight cut have odd order at least three.

For a nonbipartite graph, try every pair of distinct vertices u,v. Compute a maximum matching in H=G−u−v. If H lacks a perfect matching, its deficiency is positive and even. Compute D as the vertices whose individual deletion leaves its maximum matching size unchanged, and put A=N_H(D) outside D. The Gallai–Edmonds decomposition gives o(H−A)−|A| equal to the deficiency, where o counts odd components. With B=A∪{u,v}, the perfect matching of G gives o(G−B)≤|B|, so equality must hold and H has deficiency exactly two. In every perfect matching of G exactly one edge leaves each odd component toward B. These edges saturate B. Thus no edge inside B or from B to an even component can appear in any perfect matching. Matching coverage and connectivity exclude both types, including any even component. If all odd components were single vertices, B and its complement would be a bipartition. Choose an odd component of order at least three. Its complement contains B and at least one further odd component, so this gives a nontrivial tight shore. [Edmonds, Paths, trees, and flowers](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1965-045-4)

If H is disconnected and has a perfect matching, each component has even order. Choose one component K and return K∪{u}. In any perfect matching, either both u,v are matched into K or neither is. In either case exactly one matching edge crosses the returned shore. Both shores have order at least three. If all pair deletions give a connected graph with a perfect matching, G is bicritical and 3-connected. It is a brick by the Edmonds–Lovász–Pulleyblank characterization and has no nontrivial tight cut. Connected matching-covered graphs of order at least four are 2-connected. Alternatively any cutvertex would give a disconnecting pair for order at least six by taking a second vertex in a component of order at least two. [Edmonds, Lovász and Pulleyblank](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02579233)

For a bipartite matching-covered graph G with sides L,R of size q≥3, try deleting two vertices from each side. When deletion prevents a perfect matching, ordinary alternating reachability from all exposed left vertices of a maximum matching gives a nonempty deficient left subset S. Adding the two deleted right vertices back yields |N_G(S)|≤|S|+1. In the original graph Hall's inequality is strict for nonempty proper left subsets. Indeed, equality would saturate all neighbors in every perfect matching, making every edge from those neighbors to L outside S unusable and contradicting connectivity and matching coverage. Thus |N_G(S)|=|S|+1. Exactly one neighbor must be matched outside S in every perfect matching, giving the nontrivial tight shore S∪N_G(S). At least two left vertices are outside S. Conversely, failure of the brace neighborhood inequality gives such an S. Delete two neighbors of S and two left vertices outside S to expose the deficiency. [Brace characterization](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dam.2024.12.019)

The nonbipartite branch uses O(n²) maximum-matching calls, including O(n) extra calls in its single terminal barrier search. The optional bipartite branch uses O(n⁴) calls. Constructing and scanning an induced graph costs O(n+m) per call. The matching-hull application can handle all bipartite nodes directly. The independent checker enumerates all perfect matchings and all nontrivial odd shores on ten small multigraph fixtures.
