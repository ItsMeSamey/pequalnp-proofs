# Exactly two named vertices suffice in the all-even target

For an arbitrary mixed-loop tournament (M,d,beta) under the outgoing-row convention, append vertex r of loop one as the sole marked vertex. Direct r→z iff beta(z)=1 and replace each old loop by d(z)+1+beta(z). Any marked support now contains r; its row exactly enforces the original odd mark, and each changed old loop compensates for the edge from z to r. Thus every old witness lifts uniquely to include r. Apply the all-even buffer of tournament-even-buffer.md. Its target consists entirely of loopless directed tournament rows and exactly two compulsory vertices, r and the new p. The total increase is four vertices. Both directions preserve raw old witnesses. Multiple buffer completions can occur.

The old unique-witness family of mixed-tournament-anchor.md with singleton mark already gives a simpler obstruction to bounded witnesses: apply the buffer directly, forcing both pin p and old vertex 0. Every feasible support contains all n originals. For any desired size k of the disjoint parity mark, replace vertex 0 by a transitive k-vertex module with the same exterior orientation. In any selection of two or more clones, the last and penultimate selected clones have outgoing internal parities zero and one, with the same exterior parity. They cannot both satisfy the loopless condition. Odd marked population therefore picks exactly one clone; all other old selected vertices remain compulsory. This persists for k=2 after fixing either clone absent.

Under tournament switching by a vertex label s, a cut submatrix on U,V changes by s_U 1^T+1 s_V^T. Each direction of each individual cut changes rank by at most two. The optimized maximum directed prefix rank changes by at most two, by applying this bound to any order before taking a minimum in either direction. The forced examples have rank at most one for the natural forward cut in their original order, and at most two in reverse. Appending finitely many vertices increases these bounds only by a constant. No inference of computational hardness follows from large required supports.
