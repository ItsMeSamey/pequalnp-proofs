# Separate AND and OR lower bounds on a cycle

For the edge function of the simple cycle C_h, h≥5, every binary monotone circuit has at least h−1 OR gates. If h=2k, it also has at least k AND gates. Sharing is unrestricted. These are counts of physical gates, not formula occurrences.

## Pinning and the forest residuals

In a trimmed AND/OR circuit, an essential raw x forced to zero removes an OR whenever the restriction is nonzero. Follow a live path from x. If it contains an OR, the operand of the first OR is forced zero along the preceding AND-only path, so this physical OR simplifies. If there is no such path, then an AND-only path from x to the output makes the output identically zero at x=0. Dually, forcing an essential raw to one removes an AND when the restricted output is not identically one. Distinct gates with forced operands are simultaneously removable by forward propagation, even when one feeds the other. No new paid gates need be introduced by constant propagation.

A forest with e≥1 edges needs at least e−1 OR gates: pin leaves to zero successively until one edge remains. Each step deletes one edge; its leaf is essential and the remaining edge function is nonzero. A graph with a perfect matching and a leaf l pays one AND to pin l=1, since the assignment with all other inputs zero still gives zero. Its mate p is the only neighbor of l. Pinning p=0 next leaves exactly the graph with those two vertices removed, still perfectly matched. This reasoning applies on the last matched pair too, before its mate is pinned zero. Thus an even-order path needs at least half its order in AND gates. An odd-order path needs at least the floor of half its order, by pinning one endpoint zero and using the even path.

## A one-wire interface cannot hide a proper vertex block

Suppose all paths from the variables of S to output pass through g, and g depends only on S. Then the circuit factors as H(g(S),outside). If S contains an internal edge, evaluating the outside variables at zero gives a nonconstant monotone unary function of g, so it equals g. Consequently all singleton inputs of S give g=0, just as the empty input does. An edge from S to an outside vertex would then be indistinguishable from its outside singleton. If S instead has no internal edge and every vertex is essential, each vertex of S has an outside neighbor. Its singleton must set g=1, by the same edge comparison. Hence every vertex of S has the same outside neighbors. Every nonempty proper S of a connected graph has a crossing edge. In C_h with h≥5 no distinct vertices have identical neighborhoods: either compare their two cyclic neighbors directly, or note that two independent vertices sharing both neighbors give a four-cycle.

## The first paid gate

First count OR gates, taking AND gates free. Suppose q≤h−2. For every vertex x, setting x=0 leaves a path on h−1 vertices with h−2 edges. The forest bound above requires h−3 ORs, so specializing this x cannot eliminate two distinct OR gates.

Trim dead gates and choose a realization minimizing the paid count. Let g be its earliest live OR. Its two operands are pure AND monomials; let S be their combined raw support. Every x in S has an AND-only path into an operand of g and therefore kills that operand when pinned zero. A path from x that meets a different OR first would kill an operand of a second OR. A path to the output with no OR at all would make x necessary on every true input, whereas the cycle has an edge avoiding x. Thus every path from S to output passes through g. A singleton S would make g an alias, contrary to minimality. The one-wire interface rules out proper S of size at least two. If S is all vertices, the nonconstant output must be g itself, a union of two monomials, which has at most two minimal true sets. C_h has h. This proves q≥h−1.

Now count ANDs on C_(2k), taking ORs free. Suppose q≤k−1. Setting x=1, then setting its two neighbors zero, leaves a path on 2k−3 vertices. That path requires at least k−2 ANDs. Thus pinning x=1 alone cannot eliminate two ANDs. The earliest live AND g has two pure raw OR operands. For every x in their combined support S, a path first meeting another AND would eliminate a second AND at x=1. An all-OR path to output would make the singleton x true. Thus the same interface rules out proper nontrivial S, and a singleton S is an alias. If S is all vertices, g would compute the whole edge function as a single product of two ORs. Their supports must be disjoint because every singleton is false, so the graph would be complete bipartite, impossible for a cycle of length at least six. This proves q≥k.

## Globally at most one cycle, including attached forests

For ORs, peel off-cycle leaves at zero, one OR per deleted edge, until the unique cycle remains. Separate tree components can be peeled completely while the cycle keeps the output nonzero. The cycle bound supplies the remaining h−1 ORs; the total is at least m−1.

For ANDs, start with any perfect matching of n edges. While a leaf exists, pin it to one and its mate to zero as above. Charge one AND per removed pair, maintaining a perfect matching on the residual. If the cycle breaks, finish by the same rule on the forest. If no leaf remains before the graph is empty, the residual is just the bare cycle, since there is at most one cycle and every residual vertex is matched. Apply its k-AND bound. Total ANDs are at least n. Thus matched bipartite graphs with globally at most one cycle and no C4 satisfy the exact m+n−1 lower bound, including disconnected forests and isolated matched-edge components. This does not address multiple-cycle components. The proof is mathematical, without a Lean kernel replay.
