# Where the tie survives in a virtual two-sum tree

Take the connected canonical fundamental bipartite graph with private path L–a–b–R, where L,b are basis vertices, a,R are nonbasis vertices, the private vertices both have degree two, and LR is absent. The tie is d_L=c_R in the represented cycle/cocycle coordinates, equivalently equality of the two endpoint selectors. The three ordinary markers have no private selector coefficients.

For an ordinary-element partition X,Y, the matroid connectivity is the sum of the ranks of the two cross blocks of the fundamental adjacency, one in each orientation. Indeed the rank of X is its number of basis elements plus the rank of its nonbasis columns restricted to basis rows in Y; similarly for Y. A proper two-sum has connectivity one, so its entire cross graph is one complete bipartite graph in one orientation. In particular this test applies to every edge of a supplied tree directly in the original fundamental graph.

**List of cuts.** If L and R are separated, the only placements in path order are L | abR, La | bR, and Lab | R. The remaining placement Lb | aR gives an invertible two-by-two cross minor. The first cut is a star centered on L: a has only L on the opposite side. The last cut is dually a star centered on R. The middle cut has just the crossing edge ab, since both its endpoints have only each other as crossing neighbors. If both terminals are on the same side, the other side cannot contain both private vertices, since the two exterior path edges then cross in opposite orientations. If it contains a alone among the four, the biclique has two neighbors L,b and no other vertex on the a side adjacent to b. Thus a is its only crossing vertex and has no neighbor on its own side. Connectedness forces the other side to be the singleton a, forbidden in a proper two-sum. The argument for b is symmetric.

Consequently a,b belong to torsos on the path connecting the torsos that own L,R. They occur in the order L,a,b,R (coincidences permitted). If the two private vertices have different owners, any cut between them exhibits the central bridge.

**Central bridge gives an unconditional three-row reduction.** In the native fundamental graph the sum of selected-degree guards over either shore of the bridge cut gives x_a x_b=0. For tied terminal value one, the two private guards require that selection of either private vertex implies selection of the other. Hence both are zero. For tied value zero they can both be set to zero: only their own guards and the inactive terminal guards can change. Conversely every tied model with both private vertices deleted extends by zeros. The remaining graph has two independent blocks, each carrying a terminal with nonzero fourth (tie) label. Solve the joint four-score target (z,0) over these blocks using only three-row oracles. In four bits every pair of distinct scores is a three-row affine fiber. Test all pairs on each block. A missing pair supplies a known absent score h, so each other score s is tested exactly by the pair {s,h}. If some block meets every pair, choose a nonzero singleton score w in the other block. The pair {target,target+w} in the first block and the choice between singleton and empty in the other complete the witness. If both blocks have exact tables, convolve them.

**Common private torso.** If a,b belong to the same torso H, the two ends are represented on H by L,R themselves when local and otherwise by the first virtual element on their respective outgoing branches. Call these representatives l,r. Every crossing L-end induces equality d_l=d_L, since the triangle {L,a,b} has local half {L,virtual} on the remote side, a parallel pair. Equivalently use the standard matching traces on that two-sum; the induced two-element cycle forces equality of the two cocycle bits. The same reasoning on the triad {a,b,R} gives c_r=c_R. The trace identities apply on the assembled remote limb, so intermediate torsos add no constraints. The tie on H is exactly d_l=c_r. All three ordinary scores still sum over the tree. Untied limbs carry only their three ordinary scores plus the parent port condition (one row selecting a state). Thus if each one-port unmarked torso after conditional baseline corrections has effective ordinary rank at most two, its conditional tables require only three rows. At H the tie and three corrected score rows generally give four effective affine conditions. If their combined rank is at most three the three-row oracle suffices there too. Without this last rank condition or another certified score-cover reduction, merely placing both private vertices in one torso does not provide a three-row algorithm.

The alternative tie formulas are d_L+c_a+c_b=0, c_R+d_a+d_b=0, and c_a+d_a+c_b+d_b=0. They follow from the same triangle and triad. The last equates occupancy of a,b, since their own cycle/cocycle coordinates are disjoint. On a torso all statements concern local disjoint assignments and matching virtual states extendible in the incident limbs.

There are exactly five accepted pairs of proxy modes, writing each mode as (c,d). For tied zero the l mode is 00 or 10, and the r mode is 00 or 01. For tied one the pair is (01,10). The zero branch may also be imposed by contracting l and deleting r, with the usual score transfer through the unique fillings, or globally by deleting the apex. The nonzero branch requires both positive bits. Conditional baseline maps from unmarked limbs can use three score dimensions, hence at most three exceptional limbs.

**Separate terminal owners retain the full problem.** Given any canonical split, attach a zero-weight nonbasis leaf to L and a zero-weight basis leaf to R. A new leaf h has guard x_h x_end=0, so its contribution to the end guard vanishes on every model. Deleting the two leaves therefore projects every witness, and inserting zeroes provides a section preserving all scores. These attachments are a parallel extension at L and a series extension at R, respectively, each a proper two-sum with a three-element torso (a rank-one matroid U(1,3) for the parallel extension, or its dual U(2,3) for the series extension; U(1,3) consists of three parallel elements). The host torso uses virtual slots for L,R and the two new torsos own the real tie ends. Iteration at those ends gives arbitrary separation in the supplied decomposition tree, while the host retains both private elements. The construction realizes the original instance with the tie ends in distinct torsos. It establishes universality for that ownership pattern, independently of any conjectured hardness.
