# Recognizing factors and generated groups of an explicitly tabulated native patch

Normalize by a baseline so that the input/output positions are identified, and let T be the table containing the identity. Its finest common setwise-invariant blocks are exactly the orbits of G generated by T; they are computable as connected components of the undirected edges joining a to h(a) for all entries h and positions a. Native block factorization shows T is the full product of the restrictions to these blocks. With an explicit table one may check the identity by comparing its cardinality to the product of the cardinalities of the marginal tables; containment in the product is automatic. With a promise of native origin and supplied marginal witness masks, the block-correction algorithm reconstructs a setting for any desired combination.

The universal fixed-cap test can therefore be applied one orbit at a time. If each marginal passes, a native patch can be represented by separate abstract factors, and the composition theorem gives the external decision and settings for all factors. Either look up the resulting whole tuple or use the block-correction algorithm to recover gates. Conversely pad a failing marginal cap with disconnected return wiring on all other factors. No physical choice on other factors affects the offending marks. Thus the expensive factorial in the test may depend on the largest orbit rather than the whole number of ports, although finding a table from an arbitrary circuit is a separate task.

A further necessary condition on finite native tables follows from adjacent masks. Before a switch toggle, only the external input trajectories passing through one of its three incoming sockets can change. Hence adjacent exterior permutations differ by identity, a single transposition, or a single three-cycle. Since the mask cube is connected, its table is connected in the graph joining any two entries with such a difference. The relative input permutations of these edges generate G by telescoping along a table spanning tree. In particular a transitive native table with M entries has at most 2(M−1)+1 positions: each of the M−1 supports has at most three points, and their hypergraph is connected. This last conclusion applies orbitwise if the group is intransitive.

More precisely G is a direct product over its nontrivial orbits of full alternating or symmetric groups. Consider the relative input permutations on a connected component of that support hypergraph; each generator is a genuine individual cycle, not a simultaneous product of disjoint small cycles. If a transposition is present, grow a set X on which the generated group contains every transposition, starting from its two points. An overlapping transposition adds its new point. For an overlapping three-cycle that also includes new points, conjugating transpositions on X by that three-cycle gives transpositions joining new points to X whenever some other element of X is fixed. If X is contained in the triple (then |X|=2), conjugation of its one transposition instead gives the other two pairs of the triple. Thus grow X to the entire component.

If every generator is a three-cycle, start with one triple X and its alternating group. An overlapping three-cycle with two new points y,z can, by conjugation within X, be written as (a y z) for every a in X. The product (a y z)(b y z)^{-1} gives (a y b) for all distinct a,b in X, generating the alternating group on X together with y. For an overlapping triple with a single new point z, conjugation and inversion give all (a b z) for a,b in X: the alternating group is transitive on unordered pairs even for |X|=3, and 2-transitive when |X|≥4. These cycles generate the enlarged alternating group. Repeat until the component is exhausted. As each generator's support lies in only one component, the groups on the components are independent. The group statement concerns products of table entries; it does not imply every such product occurs as one physical setting.
