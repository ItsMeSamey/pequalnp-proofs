# From planar native switches to balanced full stars

Use a closed directed switch network with independently selectable native three-track switches, fixed wire connections, and two marked wire occurrences. At each switch its two allowed bijections between the three incoming and three outgoing flags are the two alternate perfect matchings of their six-cycle of allowed input-output pairs. The required embedding places the six flags around each vertex in that six-cycle order, up to reversal and cyclic shift. Ordinary planarity of the graph with its port names forgotten is insufficient.

To recognize such an embedding, replace each switch by a wheel on a 12-vertex rim, alternating six real flag vertices in prescribed order with six gap vertices. Give each real vertex exactly its one external wire end. Test ordinary planarity of this expanded graph. An admissible drawing extends to this wheel drawing. Conversely, in any planar drawing on the sphere a wheel has its cyclic rim and a hub on one side triangulating that side into wedges. Each wedge contains at most one real flag. If a wire from this flag entered the wedge, it would be the sole edge connecting the material in the wedge to the rest of its original connected component. Such a wire is impossible: in the even-degree original multigraph no individual wire is a bridge (the cut of any vertex subset has even size); after wheel expansion each wire still belongs to a cycle, by connecting flags along their respective rims. Disjoint components lying inside wedges can be redrawn separately. Thus the wheel disk can be replaced by one switch with the prescribed rotation. A loop has two distinct flag vertices even if its ends lie at the same switch. A directed fixed wire circle without switches can be handled directly. Two marks in different underlying components cannot join; other components can be removed. Repeat occurrences on a wire may be marked at separate interior points.

Work in the resulting connected spherical component. The even-degree skeleton admits a face checkerboarding, since its planar dual is bipartite, including in the presence of loops or parallel edges. Every facial boundary is a directed closed walk: incoming and outgoing flags alternate around each switch. The two matchings connect the two flags bordering each of the three black corners, or the two bordering each white corner. Which physical setting corresponds to black is determined locally from the labeled flags. Complement that setting locally when decoding. Independence is essential to this unrestricted choice of corners.

For every black face take a disk vertex and for every switch take a selectable star center. Connect it to that face once per black corner incident with the switch. Draw the three arms through the corresponding sectors. Within a face use distinct spokes in boundary order even if its facial walk visits a switch more than once: use the face's boundary disk with a separate preimage for every corner and then identify the boundary flags. This yields an embedded incidence multigraph with exactly three arms per switch. A digon has two boundary sides and two corners; neither is collapsed. Record the black face disk and a germ on the adjacent white face for each marked wire.

For a subset of stars take a small closed regular neighborhood of all black disk vertices and the selected full stars with all their incidences. Its boundary circles trace precisely the wire trajectories. One can realize this correspondence by first following the black facial walks just inside their faces, with one distinct marked crossing position per wire occurrence. At a selected switch attach a small central disk with three bands to the three black sectors. The boundary now takes the three white-corner turns at that switch. At every other switch it retains the black-corner turns. This local description is unaffected by repeated corners, parallel arms or a one-edge facial walk. Each marked boundary point is incident on the neighborhood side with the component of its black disk and on the other side with the component containing its designated white germ. The white germ can equivalently be a chosen interior point of its white face.

On the sphere the adjacency graph for the regions cut out by disjoint boundary circles, with one edge for each circle, is a tree: each new circle splits its containing region in two. In particular two marked boundary points are on the same circle exactly when both their neighborhood-side components and their opposite-side components agree. The former means the selected incidence graph connects the two black disks. The latter means that its complement connects the two white germs: thickening an embedded graph to a small regular neighborhood preserves the complementary face components. Sphere topology is used here; two regions on a surface of positive genus can share several boundary circles.

Take a seam between the white germs avoiding vertices, crossing arms transversely, and assign each incidence its crossing parity. A simple incidence cycle has odd gain exactly when it separates the two germs, by the Jordan curve theorem. Conversely if a finite embedded graph separates the germs it contains such a simple cycle. For example, take the mod-two boundary of the face of the graph containing one germ: counting halfedges with multiplicity gives an even edge set, and its intersection parity with the seam is one if the other germ is in a different face. Decompose this set into simple cycles, including two-edge parallel cycles. At least one crosses oddly. Hence complementary connectivity is exactly vanishing of gain on all cycles of the selected graph. If the germs coincide or lie in the same initial white face they can be represented by a common point and the zero seam.

The native answer is therefore YES exactly when some balanced full-star component connects the two black disks. From a successful mask discard every selected star outside the desired component; this preserves black connectivity and cannot separate the white germs. Conversely use only a balanced connecting component's stars and leave all others off in the star convention. Other black disks are isolated. Every retained cycle is even, so the white germs are connected. The boundary-tree assertion then gives the required single trajectory. This also handles equal root disks with no star needed. The polynomial [annular gain-chain algorithm](README.md) now gives a mask; map its star bits back to the two physical matchings and trace the witness. This result concerns planar networks with the prescribed six-port rotations and independent gate controls.
