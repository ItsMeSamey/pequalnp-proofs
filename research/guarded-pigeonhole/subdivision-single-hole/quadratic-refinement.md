# Refining a bag after affine absorption

For a consistent affine parameter space P and Boolean quadratic test rows Q, repeatedly replace P by the common zero set of all affine functions in the span of Q restricted to P. This is an affine subspace computed by row elimination; an empty subspace rejects. A nonempty fixed point P* has no nonzero affine function in that restricted row span. Its residual quadratic rank is the dimension of the image of the restricted row span modulo affine functions.

This construction is canonical. If T⊆P is nonempty and stable for the same test rows, then T survives every reduction of P: each affine consequence restricts to an affine combination of test rows on T, which vanishes identically by stability. Thus P* is the largest stable subspace when the process succeeds. In particular if one first imposes additional affine pins, any resulting nonempty fixed point is contained in the original P*. Its quadratic rank cannot exceed that of P*: restriction induces a linear map on functions modulo affine functions. These conclusions refer to actual pinning on the same system.

For a parent bag with fixed external demand, fix also the two child partial demands summing to it. The underlying affine space becomes a product on the two children's coordinates. Assume every pair test belongs entirely to one child. On the product nonconstant quadratic monomials from different children cannot cancel against one another. Hence a sum of local test combinations is affine precisely if both local combinations are affine. Since the local equations themselves are present, the global affine closure removes exactly the two local sets of consequences. Induction gives a product of fixed points when both are nonempty, or an affine contradiction if either is empty. In the nonempty case the residual rank is the sum of the two local ranks. By monotonicity under the intermediate pins this sum is at most the original stabilized parent rank.

Thus a proposed split has a polynomial-time filter. For each pair of compatible linear demands, run affine absorption on both children before computing any Walsh sums. Keep it only if both stabilized spaces are nonempty. Even if a surviving child system has no Boolean solution detectable beyond affine absorption, its rank still satisfies the sum bound. At separator dimension b a parent state has at most 2^b split states when one child's boundary dimension is at most b. This controls a supplied split; it does not construct a best decomposition.

Without the filter, child ranks can be arbitrarily larger. Give child A the equations u_i v_i=0 and (u_i+h)v_i=0 for i=1,...,m and child B the two equations (h+1)y=0 and (h+1)(y+1)=0. The common interface is h. Parent affine reduction forces h=1 and then v_i=0, with final rank zero. At h=0 child A instead retains m independent products u_i v_i while B is affine-inconsistent. The h=1 split survives with ranks zero on both children.

These rows have a planar graphic realization with disjoint forbidden edge pairs. Use a planar potential graph with fixed O=0, vertex H of potential h, and U_i,V_i,Y of their named potentials. In A use one O–U_i edge, one H–U_i edge, and two O–V_i edges. In B use two O–H edges and two O–Y edges. Flip the two O–H biases and one O–Y bias in a particular dual edge set and use its boundary as the prescribed demand in the plane dual graph. The cut-space parameters of the potential graph give the homogeneous joins of the dual, yielding exactly the displayed factors after translation. Both connected pieces meet at O and H, so the overlap dimension is one.
