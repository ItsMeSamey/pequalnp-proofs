# Unbounded maximum degree with a bounded vertex cover of high–high edges

Let G be a simple graph, t the number of binary vertex-gain coordinates, k the size of a supplied vertex cover T of the edges whose endpoints both have degree at least three. For fixed k,t there is a deterministic polynomial-time decision and witness algorithm for prescribed-score induced even subgraphs. The exponent depends on k+t. Use f(d) from odd-edge-witness.md.

First enumerate at most t selected isolated vertices I sufficient to give the total gain of all original isolates, and remove their closed neighborhood. In a correct branch the remaining witness has no isolates. Classify vertices by residual degree, at least three for H and at most two for low vertices. The surviving cover restricted to H still covers H–H edges, as degrees did not increase. Guess U, the active subset of this cover, fix other cover vertices off. All high vertices outside the cover form an independent set P. Divide P according to the parity of the number of adjacent U vertices.

For the even class, any unused vertex can be turned on without violating its own guard; no active low vertex neighbors it because every active low vertex has two selected neighbors. Toggling it affects its score and the parity at its neighbors in U. Mark it by that combined gain of dimension d=t+|U|. Keep at most d formerly omitted even-class vertices omitted with the same total combined gain as the formerly omitted family. Turn on the rest. This preserves the total score and U parities. Guess those at most d omissions. The hitherto unknown high statuses are exactly the odd class.

Consider the selected edges touching low vertices in this witness, excluding high–high edges. Pair incidences arbitrarily at each active even-class vertex (its residual degree is even) and at each active low vertex (its degree is two). Cutting at every occurrence of a vertex in U or in the odd class leaves edge-disjoint trails between such endpoints and closed trails with no such endpoint. A low vertex occurs in one trail exactly, since its two incident edges are paired.

Encode each trail by one abstract edge on its odd-class endpoints. For each endpoint in U, use a distinct dummy leaf instead. A closed trail with no endpoint becomes a separate edge between two new leaves. Repeated endpoints can create loops. Give the abstract edge the vertex gains of its internal low vertices, and for each odd-class endpoint h add the vertex gain of h and one flux coordinate at each neighbor of h in U. For each endpoint at u in U add its unit flux at u. Thus the gain has dimension d. With all abstract edges selected, each occurring odd-class vertex has odd abstract degree: it originally saw an odd number of U neighbors and was selected with even total degree. Distinct dummy leaves have degree one. The abstract all-odd gain therefore has a witness of at most f(d) abstract edges.

Unfold these trails. Fixed even-class vertices retain even low degree; any used low vertex retains its two edges and the complete selected neighbor set. An odd-class vertex is activated exactly when it has positive abstract degree, which is odd, so its gain and its incident high-edge flux at U are correctly included. The d-dimensional gain guarantees the original score and all U guard parities after counting the fixed high states and fixed high–high edges. Therefore at most 2f(d) odd-class vertices need be active in this branch.

Enumerate that active set. All high vertices now have fixed selection status. Delete inactive high vertices, force high–high edges between selected endpoints, impose even edge degree over F2, and represent each low vertex's nonisolated status by one fixed incident edge (or zero for degree zero). All remaining equations for guards and score are linear. The output is obtained by adding the specified high vertices, including those now isolated, and the initial I. Each selected low uses both incident edges, so every induced edge is present. This also proves soundness of arbitrary successful guesses without knowing the existential trail decomposition.

The combined runtime is bounded by 2^k n^(2t+k+2f(t+k)+O(1)) times a function of the parameters. No bound on maximum degree, number of high vertices, treewidth, or bipartiteness is assumed. This is an XP theorem, not an unrestricted four-marker algorithm.

## Symmetric affine guard offsets

If selected v requires degree congruent to a prescribed bit b_v, use the following definition of high. For b_v=0 take vertices of degree at least three; for b_v=1 take vertices of degree at least two. The parameter k is a cover of high–high edges under this definition. Only score-compressed isolates with b_v=0 are admissible. Every other selected low vertex uses all its ambient incident edges, two for an even low and one for an odd low. The high classification may be recomputed after the initial closed-neighborhood deletion and then frozen.

For h outside the cover use residual parity b_h plus the parity of its neighbors in U. A residual-even high can be activated when it has no selected low neighbors, with the same combined-gain/flux accounting as above. A residual-odd selected high has odd degree among the retained low edges. At an odd low's single incidence terminate the trail with a private dummy leaf, including this low's gain once at its endpoint. Even low vertices remain paired and contribute once as interiors. In the final linear system each selected high vertex has required parity b_v; each even low has parity zero; each odd low of degree at most one may take its incident edge freely. A low vertex contributes the gain times an incident edge bit, or zero in degree zero. This proves the corresponding XP(t+k) bound with symmetric arbitrary guard offsets.

For a supplied decomposition A=S+UV^T with S symmetric and r columns in U,V, branch on the r-bit value y=V^T x, add these r score rows, and use offsets b+Uy with S's diagonal absorbed into them. Alternatively, if d vertices cover the unequal directed pairs, guess their bits, delete them, and add the guard rows of the selected guessed vertices to the marker system. These transfers are useful only when each resulting symmetric problem has the required structural cover bound.
