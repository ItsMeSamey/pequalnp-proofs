# Eighteen gates in the disjoint-input monotone model

We consider monotone circuits of arbitrary depth and fanout with at most two inputs per gate. At each AND gate the two operand cones must have disjoint sets of raw variables. After constant propagation and removal of copies, all live signals are false on the all-zero input. The target is the monotone graph edge function for official 9822 on ten vertices.

Trace the OR-only cone feeding the output. Each leaf of that cone is an AND product: a raw leaf would incorrectly accept its vertex singleton. Discard products accepting no two-vertex inputs. For a retained product to accept an edge, one endpoint must occur in each of its two disjoint cones. On that input the two operands see singleton assignments. Conversely the cross-product of their singleton supports is accepted by the product, hence by the output. Replacing each operand by the union of its singleton support therefore creates a collection of bicliques covering exactly the graph edges. The resulting monotone function agrees on all inputs, because its minterms and the target's minterms are those same edges.

This replacement costs no additional gates even with shared wires. For every original wire compute its singleton support by keeping its raw or OR operation and replacing every AND by zero. All such ANDs are singleton-cold because their two operand cones are disjoint. Only the OR operations used in this projected network require actual gates. The original output OR cone is disjoint from these paid ORs: every one of its wires has empty singleton support, since any true singleton there would be accepted by the output. The number of output ORs is at least t−1 for t distinct retained products, by binary connectedness. Thus we may replace the output cone with a binary OR of those t products. Bypass zeroes and singleton duplicates for free.

The given graph has no four-cycle, so every nonempty biclique is a star. Pick a center for each (either endpoint is permissible on a single-edge star). If two have the same center, union their neighborhood signals with at most one new OR, remove one AND, and reduce the final collector by one OR. Continue until distinct centers remain. No nontrivial shared OR can now serve two neighborhood operands: its two distinct raw leaves would be common neighbors of the distinct centers and would create a four-cycle. The central raw inputs themselves require no OR. A neighborhood of s vertices therefore needs at least s−1 private ORs. With t centers the total is at least sum(s−1)+t+(t−1) = sum(s)+t−1. The stars cover thirteen edges, and their centers form a vertex cover of size at least six, giving eighteen. Assigning each edge to one of six covering vertices attains eighteen by the corresponding star circuit.

The argument does not automatically include unrestricted monotone formulas. A variable may occur in both children of an AND, and it can then pass an already quadratic term through the product instead of generating that term from its two singleton supports. It also leaves unrestricted NOT gates open.
