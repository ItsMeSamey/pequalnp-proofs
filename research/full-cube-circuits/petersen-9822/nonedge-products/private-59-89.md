# Two endpoint-positive guards cannot survive an OR-optimal four-vertex path

In the outer-seven private two-hot model of private-or-pair.md, let s=5 or 8 and let the second hot raw be 9. The unique exterior positive raw reader is U_s=s AND P_s, followed by its unique OR successor V_s. On the zero-clamped outer circuit P_s accepts each singleton neighbor of s: U_s must transmit s on the corresponding edge. All three neighbors differ from both owners.

Setting s=0 loses exactly H, U_s, V_s by the signed fourteen-gate floor for G−s. Thus its circuit has six ANDs, seven ORs and one NOT directly on 9. P_s is a surviving binary gate: at z=0 it accepts three distinct neighbor singletons and rejects the empty input, so it is neither a raw nor H, and by chronology precedes the other two collapsed sites. On then setting 9=0 the graph is an eight-vertex path P8, requiring six monotone ORs by the forest bound. At most one of the seven ORs may therefore be lost.

In the single-deletion circuit the inverse of 9 has no OR reader. Such a reader would be forced one at 9=0. Every positive path from 9 avoiding its inverse meets an OR along its initial AND-only segment (otherwise the nonconstant zero cofactor would vanish), and its first OR folds on the same pin. If this were always the same negative reader, every positive influence from 9 would pass through a gate already one at 9=0: together with the separately cut decreasing inverse, the output could not increase. A second OR loss is excluded. Therefore all inverse readers are AND. At the all-zero input of the single-deletion circuit the sole true raw-source signal is the inverse itself: the earliest true binary gate would have to directly OR that signal or AND it with itself. The former was just excluded and the latter bypasses a gate in an exact fourteen-site minimum.

In the double deletion the value at P_s remains true at each neighbor singleton, by monotonicity of the original outer signals as the formal inverter output increases from zero to one with s=9=0. It remains false at zero by the preceding paragraph. We may keep unused ANDs when following this signal. Do not discard unused gates during the following zero pins; constant folding along paths to the actual output alone suffices to count lost ORs. Zeroing 9 loses at least one OR on a positive path, so at most six remain even with all other unaffected ORs retained. After folding away the clamped inverse signal, this is an ordinary positive circuit: the AND readers bypass the constant one, and other constant wires from pins can be removed as usual.

For s=5 the path in order is 0,4,3,8,6,1,2,7, and P_s is true on singletons 0 and 8 (also 7). Zero the last four vertices successively, each time deleting an endpoint of the current path. Each zero pin loses at least one OR, by essentiality and nonzero residual. The four-vertex path left at the start has at most two ORs; the P_s signal, interpreted through any aliases, is still true on each of its opposite endpoint singletons and false at zero. The case s=8 follows by the graph reflection interchanging (0,3),(1,2),(5,8),(6,7) and fixing 4,9.

We show no such signal can exist in a homogeneous monotone circuit with two ORs computing ab OR bc OR cd, even allowing unused gates. A signal true at both endpoint singletons a and d implies an OR with that property: tracing backwards through an AND retains the property in either operand, and no single raw has it. There must be exactly two ORs by the forest lower bound. Call them X,Y in chronological order. X is a disjunction of two raw conjunctions, Y of two conjunctions of raws and X. The output is a conjunction of raws, X and Y. There can be no raw factor, since the three required edges have empty intersection. An empty conjunction or constant cannot appear as a nontrivial live term in this homogeneous representation.

If X is positive at a and d, then X=a OR d and cannot be an output factor because it misses bc. The output must be Y. Its term accepting bc cannot contain X and must equal bc (a smaller conjunction would accept a false singleton). The other term must accept both ab and cd. Their supports are disjoint, so this term has no raw factor; it cannot be X or a constant because those also accept negatives. If instead Y is true at both endpoint singletons, the output must use Y (or Y would be unnecessary at the two-OR floor) and must also use X to reject those singletons. Thus X is false on a and on d separately. No term of Y accepting either of these singletons can use X. Its two terms must therefore be the raw literals a and d, implying Y misses bc. Both cases contradict the P4 target. This excludes 59 and 89 under the stated private outer-seven hypotheses.

The identical obstruction excludes 56 and its reflection 78. For 56 take s=5; the fourteen-gate G−5 circuit in this case directly inverts 6. The double-deletion tree on eight vertices has edges 12,27,79,94,43,38,40, hence the same six-OR floor. The negative-reader and all-zero arguments above use only that floor, so still apply with 6 in place of 9. Its P_5 signal remains positive on singletons 0,8. Pinning 1,2,7,9 zero in that order removes four successive leaves, losing four ORs while leaving 0,4,3,8 with the same two positive endpoint singletons. The P4 obstruction completes the contradiction. Reflecting gives 78.
