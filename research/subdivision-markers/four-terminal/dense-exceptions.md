# Gauging gains onto low-rank exceptional columns

Work after terminal contraction with binary columns q_i, gains g_i in a fixed binary label space D, and a requested circuit gain t. Given a linear map phi from the span of the columns to D, replace every gain by g'_i=g_i+phi(q_i). This preserves every circuit gain because circuit columns sum to zero. Suppose the resulting nonzero gains occur only in an exceptional set E of column rank at most three. Write A for its complement.

A circuit meeting both parts uses an independent subset J of E, because J is a proper subset of a circuit. Thus |J|<=3. Enumerate every such subset having adjusted gain t. Restrict the ordinary matroid to A union J and test for a single circuit containing all members of J. No unguessed exceptional member remains, and all elements of A have gain zero, so any returned circuit is sound. For nonempty independent J it necessarily also uses A. With |J|<=3 these are the represented binary three-terminal algorithm or its simpler one-/two-terminal cases. If t=0, also test whether A is dependent with J empty. Circuits lying wholly in E have size at most four; enumerate these subsets, test minimal dependence directly and check their gain. Membership, rank and gauge transformations are ordinary binary linear algebra. A decision-only rooted test recovers a circuit by greedily deleting ordinary nonterminals while preserving a YES answer.

The promise is recognizable in polynomial time. Enumerate spans W of at most three actual columns, including the zero subspace. Enlarge the possible exceptional set to every column lying in W and solve the linear equations phi(q_i)=g_i outside W. A solution yields the promised gauge. Conversely a minimum basis of any valid E is among the guesses, and every column outside its span had to become gain zero. This treatment covers loops and parallel columns; the sensitive cases J empty and circuits entirely of exceptional columns require the separate tests above.

## Larger fixed exceptional rank with low interface

With a fixed bound d on the rank of E, the same proof uses O(n^d) candidate independent subsets J and O(n^(d+1)) exceptional-only subsets. A rooted test on A union J still reduces to at most three roots whenever dim(span J intersect span A)<=3. One sufficient recognizable condition for a given E is dim(span E intersect span A)<=3. For independent nonempty J set u=sum J and L=span J intersect span A. Reject that J if u is absent from L. Otherwise replace J by any basis of L summing to u. For a subset X of A, having span X intersect span J exactly the line spanned by u is equivalent to having its intersection with L be this line. A minimal expression of u in X together with J then gives the required circuit, and conversely a circuit gives this trace condition. Thus basis replacement preserves existence. For general d also test the sufficient global interface bound after choosing the enlarged exceptional set. Enlarging E to all columns of its span cannot increase that bound, since the exceptional span stays W and the complementary span can only shrink. Thus the existence of a gauge and exception set satisfying both bounds is recognizable by the same span enumeration.

The binary three-terminal polynomial algorithm is described in Section 2 of [Geelen, Gerards and Whittle](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.20960v1#S2). The four-root problem without structural assumptions is not resolved here.

An independent finite audit compared the low-rank-exception criterion with literal minimal-dependency enumeration for all 16 target labels on 250 seeded binary systems, including zero and repeated columns.
