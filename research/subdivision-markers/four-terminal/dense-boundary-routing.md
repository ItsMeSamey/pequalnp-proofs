# Completing a binary circuit on a dense graphic host

Let G be a finite undirected host whose ordinary columns are the binary edge incidence vectors. Fix the other columns that must participate in the circuit, indexed by J, with p=|J|≥1. This statement is in incidence coordinates. If columns have extra coordinates vanishing on every graphic edge, first require their total outside-incidence coordinate to vanish, and include the fixed coordinate rows in the rank test below. Write σ(v) for the p-bit row of the chosen columns at v and H for the vertices where this row has odd weight. Require |H| even. All row operations below are over the binary field.

For a forest F of odd boundary H, take the components of (V,F), including isolated vertices. Form one row per component by summing its vertex signatures. A coefficient vector on J admits an expression in F precisely when its dot product with every aggregate row and every fixed extra coordinate row is zero. The reason is that the incidence span of a tree consists of all even vectors on its vertices, and the forest expression is unique. The all-ones vector on J extends to the all-ones expression on F. Thus F together with J is a circuit exactly when the row rank is p−1. Every possible completing edge set is already a forest, since any graphic cycle would constitute a proper dependence.

## Complete host

Pair H by a perfect matching M in the complete graph on H (the empty matching is allowed). The necessary and sufficient condition is that some M satisfies

    rank(fixed rows, {σ(v): v outside H}, {σ(u)+σ(v): uv in M}) = p−1.

Indeed, every forest component contains evenly many H vertices. Pair them internally. Its aggregate is the sum of the matching rows there and the signatures of its vertices outside H. The matching test therefore retains full rank from any forest witness. Conversely the edges of the matching themselves give a forest realizing precisely the tested rows. On an arbitrary host the first implication still holds. For a decision procedure, choose p−1 independent rows with distinct singleton witnesses and mutually disjoint matched endpoints. Any remaining even set of H vertices pairs freely. Guessing witnesses costs at most n^{2(p−1)} times a parameter factor. Alternatively enumerate at most 2(p−1) signature types and check that their multiplicities fit the available vertex counts. This takes f(p)poly(input size). If an unspecified subset of h extra columns may accompany the four roots, enumerate their choices when h counts the columns; if only their rank h is bounded, enumerate independent choices of at most h columns because any proper subset of a circuit is independent. In the latter case this costs at most sum_{j≤h} binomial(m,j) branches for m extras.

## Common neighbors

Suppose every two nonadjacent vertices have more than 3(p−1) common neighbors. Choose a basis of p−1 rows from the matching certificate. Reserve its singleton witnesses and the endpoints of its selected matching pairs. Fixed rows need no vertices. Each selected pair already adjacent can be isolated as an edge. For a missing pair uv let R be the span of all other current basis rows, including the desired rows for future repairs. Select a linear functional λ zero on R and one on σ(u)+σ(v). Take a common neighbor w outside all other reservations. Each other basis row has at most three reserved vertices, and the pair's own endpoints are never common neighbors. The density hypothesis guarantees an available w.

* If w is in H, precisely one of σ(u)+σ(w) and σ(v)+σ(w) evaluates to one under λ. Isolate the corresponding edge and release the unused old endpoint.
* If w is outside H and λσ(w)=1, reserve w as an isolated vertex and release both old endpoints.
* Otherwise isolate the path u,w,v. Its row evaluates to one under λ.

Replace the old basis row by the selected row. Independence persists and the gadgets have disjoint vertex sets. Each gadget removes zero or two vertices of H and has the correct odd boundary there. Delete the reserved vertices temporarily. At most 3(p−1) were removed, so any two remaining nonneighbors still have a common neighbor. The nonempty residual graph is connected. On a spanning tree the remaining even boundary has a unique edge-set expression, which can be constructed by processing leaves. Joining that forest to the protected gadgets preserves all basis rows and gives the required circuit. For p=1 connectedness alone suffices. The constructive proof assumes unlabelled graphic edges in these coordinates.

## Disjoint connected dominating sets

The same transfer holds given 3(p−1)+1 pairwise vertex-disjoint connected dominating sets. Reserve at the outset every singleton and pair endpoint in the chosen basis. At most 2(p−1) dominating sets meet these initial reservations. Allocate one further clean set to each missing pair and leave one additional clean set for final completion. These allocations are disjoint by the stated bound. A missing pair u,v has a simple u-to-v path with all internal vertices in its private connected dominating set. With λ as above, if an internal off-H vertex has value one, reserve it as an isolated singleton and release u,v. Otherwise every internal off-H vertex has value zero. In the sequence of H vertices along the path, the two end values differ. Choose consecutive H vertices of differing values, and isolate the entire intervening subpath. Its aggregate evaluates to one, and its only odd vertices are its endpoints. Release all old endpoints outside that subpath. Each new gadget is disjoint from future endpoints and all other private sets. Deleting the protected gadgets and singletons preserves the untouched connected dominating set, so the residual graph is connected and the same spanning-tree completion applies. This sufficient condition is conditional on the sets existing; constructing the circuit via this proof uses a supplied packing.

## Sparse counterexample

Let the ordinary host be a path on five vertices with four successive independent edge columns e_1,...,e_4. Take as roots r_i the sum of the other three e_j. They form a basis since the four-by-four binary matrix I+11ᵀ is its own inverse. Their sum is the sum of the four path edges, with boundary the two end vertices. In the completed host the direct edge between the ends gives a circuit with the four roots: it equals their sum and no proper subsum equals it. In the actual host the only expression for that boundary uses all four path edges. Its union with the roots contains the proper dependence consisting of r_1 and its three path edges. Hence the matching criterion alone gives a false positive on this host.
