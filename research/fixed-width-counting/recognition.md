# Recovering long-clause orders with arbitrary exceptional clauses

Fix a constant short width k. Split each wider, non-tautological clause into its positive and its negative support. Let h be the fewest such clauses whose removal gives the consecutive-ones property separately in each of the two incidence matrices. The two column orders may differ. Both the exceptional set and the orders can be found approximately in polynomial time, retaining the native fixed-width counting exponent when h=O(n).

For a binary matrix with m rows make one row vertex per row and one marker vertex per column. Column j induces a clique on its marker and all incident rows. The union is the entire graph. Every marker is simplicial, with its closed neighborhood a maximal clique. An interval order for the matrix represents each nonempty row by the interval between its endpoints, with markers represented by points; empty rows may be isolated. Conversely, take an interval graph's consecutive ordering of maximal cliques, retaining just the distinct marked cliques. Each row contains precisely its original marked columns and still occurs consecutively. Deleting a row commutes with this graph construction.

Replace each marker by 8m+1 true twins. Deleting all rows gives an interval graph. Deleting r rows that solve the matrix problem also solves the expanded graph, since duplicating intervals realizes the twins. Cao's polynomial 8-approximation for interval vertex deletion therefore returns at most 8m vertices and at most eight times the optimum row-deletion number. Each marker group survives. Discard the returned marker deletions: restoring copies of a surviving true twin preserves interval representability. Run this algorithm separately for the two signs and take the union of the deleted rows. Its size is at most 16h. Obtain both orders by consecutive-ones recognition of the surviving rows. Empty rows and columns pose no obstruction.

Concatenate the two signed orders. Each surviving clause has at most two intervals. Apply the linear-variable bounded-width compiler in intervals.md, leave original short clauses in place, and apply a free selector for each exceptional clause, gated by the falsity of every literal in that clause. The difference of even-selector and odd-selector counts filters out assignments falsifying any exceptional clause, by the binomial theorem. A uniquely extending parity chain costs O(h) more variables. There are O(n+h) variables, constant width and polynomial input size. A fixed-width counting algorithm with bit bound poly(L) times 2^{O(N loglog N/sqrt(log N))} consequently gives this same bound for N=n+h (with usual constant-size conventions). The bound permits any regular row count allowed by the input-reading factor. A synthetic high-density class takes all pairwise unions of one of linearly many equal-length sliding intervals from each sign on disjoint alphabets, with an arbitrary bounded-width core and arbitrary exceptional rows. The regular rows are incomparable and can have quadratic cardinality.

This is an application of existing recognition and approximation methods. Booth–Lueker give linear-time C1P recognition (https://doi.org/10.1145/800116.803776). Dom, Guo and Niedermeier study consecutive ones submatrix modification (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcss.2009.07.001). Cao explicitly states the polynomial ratio-eight general interval vertex deletion algorithm on p. 3 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.1515). The selector construction appears in Wu–Wang §4 (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.02560v1#S4). No literature priority is claimed.
