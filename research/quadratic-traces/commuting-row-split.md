# Constructing a shared row direction from commuting polar operators

Let A be a nonsingular alternating bilinear form on an n-dimensional space over F. For the other alternating differences B_i define T_i by B_i(u,v)=A(T_i u,v); these operators are self-adjoint. A half-dimensional A-isotropic space Y is simultaneously isotropic for all B_i exactly when all T_i preserve Y, since Y=Y^perp. Assume a nonsingular A is supplied or has been found in the difference span. The conclusions below do not give a general algorithm for finding a nonsingular member of an arbitrary matrix space.

**Commuting theorem.** If the T_i commute, a simultaneous invariant Lagrangian exists over every perfect field of characteristic two and over every field of other characteristic. There is a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm over a finite field specified with efficient field arithmetic; a general perfect field needs effective square roots in characteristic two to obtain the algorithmic version. No alternation condition on square-free products is needed.

Let R be the unital matrix algebra generated by the T_i. It has a basis of at most n² matrices computed by closing a linear basis under generator multiplication. When R is commutative, every S∈R is self-adjoint. Call v good if A(v,Sv)=0 for every S∈R. A good v generates an invariant isotropic subspace Rv because A(Pv,Qv)=A(v,PQv). Outside characteristic two every vector is good since a self-adjoint operator S with a skew form A satisfies A(v,Sv)=-A(v,Sv). In characteristic two the quadratic expression for each basis S has only square terms, since the cross terms cancel. Over a perfect field take inverse Frobenius of their coefficients and solve the resulting linear system. Over GF(2^e) square roots are computed by raising to 2^(e-1).

The common kernel is nonzero whenever V is nonzero. In characteristic two, take any nonzero simple R-submodule M (it exists by finite dimension). As an R-module it is the regular rank-one module of a finite field extension K=R/m for a maximal ideal m. Choose v generating M. The restricted pairing is of the form A(xv,yv)=λ(xy) for an F-linear λ:K→F by self-adjointness; the restriction may be degenerate. Alternation gives λ(z²)=0 for all z∈K. The finite extension K of a perfect field is perfect, hence λ=0. Every member of M is good. This existence argument needs no algorithm for finding a simple module.

Take any computed nonzero good v, set L=Rv, and pass to L^perp/L. Both L and L^perp are invariant, and the quotient pairing is nonsingular alternating. Repeat, lifting the result to V. Dimension drops at each iteration; the end result is an invariant isotropic subspace of dimension n/2. All construction steps use polynomially many field operations.

**Noncommuting extension.** It suffices that the two-sided ideal I of R generated by the commutators [T_i,T_j] be nilpotent. Both I and nilpotence are recognizable in polynomial time by closing the commutators under left/right generator products and computing successive ideal powers in the ≤n²-dimensional algebra until zero or stabilization. Let W=ann_V(I); this space is nonzero when V≠0 by nilpotence, and it is R-invariant since I is a two-sided ideal. On W the induced operators commute. Restricted to W, every R action is self-adjoint for the possibly degenerate restriction of A: words move adjointly to their reversals, which agree on W. Thus the previous linear good-vector construction works on W even if A|W is degenerate. A good cyclic subspace L⊂W is isotropic in the original V and invariant under the full algebra. The quotient L^perp/L inherits nilpotence of the image of I; recurse. This returns the same balanced row split. Over finite fields, if I is not nilpotent the same procedure may still succeed when the required annihilators are nonzero along the chosen sequence, but the sufficient theorem asserts no general guarantee in that case.

**Examples separating the tests.**

* Over GF(2), take the four-dimensional commutative algebra D=F[u,v]/(u²,v²), acting by multiplication on itself. The form A(x,y)=[uv](xy) is nonsingular and alternating; x² is constant. The forms associated to multiplication by u and v are alternating, but A(1,uv·1)=1. Thus the square-free-product alternation criterion fails. The ideal (u)=span(u,uv) is nevertheless an invariant Lagrangian. The example works in all characteristic-two fields.
* For a strict noncommuting instance take W⊕W*, A((x,f),(y,g))=g(x)−f(y), and T_S=diag(S,Sᵀ) in a dual basis. Choose the two 3×3 elementary matrices S=E₁₂,E₂₃. Their commutator on the first block is E₁₃; products show that its two-sided ideal in the represented algebra is nilpotent. The operators do not commute. For S=E₁₂,E₂₁ on dimension two the commutator has nonzero powers (its first block is diag(1,−1)), so the ideal is not nilpotent. The evident first summand is still an invariant Lagrangian. Each B_S is alternating in either characteristic.
* Imperfect-field boundary: over F=GF(2)(s,t) let K=F(√s,√t), dimension four, and let λ pick the coefficient of √(st) in its standard F-basis. Use A(x,y)=λ(xy) on K. It is nonsingular since K is a field and λ≠0, and alternating since K²⊂F. Multiplication by √s and √t gives commuting self-adjoints whose associated forms are alternating. An invariant nonzero F-subspace under both is a K-submodule of K, hence all of K. There is no simultaneous invariant Lagrangian and hence no balanced common isotropic split. This is an explicit reason for the perfect-field condition.

The regular single-operator case relates to the established theory of pairs of alternating forms [Gow–Laffey](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-3795(84)90139-3). No priority claim is made for these linear-algebra statements. Their application here is deterministic balanced-split construction for the row-wise phase compiler. An existing Lagrangian guarantees equality of all restricted polar parts when A,B_i span the relevant differences; other affine support and residual cost assumptions in the compiler still apply.
