# How many witness bits can a quadratic SAT verifier need?

A witness-preserving quadratic representation for a Boolean predicate F on q inputs uses b auxiliary bits and any finite collection of degree-at-most-two polynomials over F₂. It satisfies F(x)=1 exactly when some y in {0,1}^b makes all equations Q_j(x,y)=0 hold. Allow nonuniform equations, any number of them, unlimited reuse, and many possible witnesses. The following linear-size circuit refutes a universal o(q) bound on b even under that freedom.

Take q=3m, divide the inputs into three m-bit strings x,y,z and set f(x,y,z)=∑_i x_i y_i z_i in F₂. It has O(q) ordinary binary gates. Its YES density is p_m=(1−(3/4)^m)/2 by independence over triples. For every quadratic P on the 3m bits,
     
    |E (−1)^{f+P}| ≤ (3/4)^{m/2}.

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the average over x,y of the mean sign over z. Squaring the latter mean introduces independent z,z′. For fixed z,z′, the remaining phase in x,y is ∑_i (z_i+z′_i)x_i y_i plus an affine form: all purely x,y quadratic terms of P cancel. For each nonzero coordinate of z+z′, averaging (−1)^{xy+ax+by} over two independent binary bits has magnitude 1/2. The other coordinates contribute at most one in magnitude. Thus the squared correlation is bounded by E_h 2^{−wt(h)}=(3/4)^m. This is a specific elementary generalized-inner-product correlation estimate. [Viola and Wigderson, Norms, XOR Lemmas, and Lower Bounds for Polynomials and Protocols](https://theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v004a007/)

The indicator of the common zero set V of any number of quadratic polynomials P_j is the average, over uniform binary vectors a, of the quadratic signs (−1)^{∑a_j P_j}. The correlation estimate and the triangle inequality give |E 1_V (−1)^f|≤(3/4)^{m/2}. If V contains only YES inputs, the left side is its density. Any cover of all YES inputs by such varieties therefore needs at least p_m(4/3)^{m/2} members, even when the defining quadratic parts of different members can differ. In an existential quadratic representation each assignment of the b witness bits gives a variety contained in YES by soundness, and their union covers YES by completeness. Hence

    b ≥ (m/2) log₂(4/3) + log₂ p_m.

Conversely m auxiliary bits suffice: require u_i=x_i y_i for each i and ∑u_i z_i=1. The auxiliary order Θ(q) is thus sharp on this family. Adding any number of extra input bits forced to zero makes the YES density arbitrarily small while keeping the same lower bound after those bits are pinned. This is a lower bound on representations preserving the set of original satisfying inputs. The circuit's decision problem and many witnesses are elementary. In particular the result gives no lower bound against decision-only equisatisfiable simplification.

**An applicable SAT algorithm.** Normalize any finite binary gate basis to XOR, constants and AND with constant overhead (NOT is addition of one). Maintain each computed signal as an explicit Boolean polynomial of degree at most two on the primary and introduced variables. At a product, form its exact squarefree product through degree four. If it has degree at most two keep it. Otherwise replace each degree-two operand signal by a fresh bit, recording the quadratic equality to its current polynomial, and use that bit for its future occurrences. The new product has degree at most two. An earlier consumer's already computed polynomial remains valid by the newly recorded equality. Handle a repeated operand once. Final acceptance becomes a quadratic equation; when an explicit final conjunction is available it can instead be split into checks on its operands. Polynomial arithmetic and all recorded equations take polynomial time in input and auxiliary lengths. Equality definitions are acyclic in order of introduction, so each x has a unique consistent lift before the output check. This handles unique solutions as well as dense accepting sets.

For a polynomial-size family where this procedure or another efficiently supplied witness-preserving encoding gives b≤ηq variables, apply the deterministic low-degree equation-counting algorithm of Lokshtanov, Paturi, Tamaki, Williams and Yu. For degree two over F₂ it gives a fixed exponential saving in the total variable count: write its bound as 2^{(1−ε)(q+b)+o(q+b)} times polynomial input overhead, for some ε>0. If (1−ε)(1+η)<1 the result is a deterministic 2^{(1−Ω(1))q} SAT algorithm on this class. Output constraints such as a conjunction pinning many original bits can have b=0 despite a single positive witness. The proven lower coefficient for f excludes a universal o(q) witness budget. It makes no comparison with an unspecified numerical ε and does not rule out faster treatment of the resulting quadratic system by another method. [Lokshtanov et al., Beating Brute Force for Systems of Polynomial Equations over Finite Fields](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1.9781611974782.143)
