# A classical factoring test for an explicit unary witness candidate

Put n=2^m and N=2^n+1. Consider the arithmetic conjecture that no deterministic machine outputs the complete factorization of every such N in time polynomial in n. Here n+1 is the bit length of N. An open lower bound is the premise, not the outcome of the construction. By unique factorization and deterministic primality testing, the ordered prime factors with repetitions are uniquely checkable in polynomial time. A self-delimiting sorted list has O(n) bits since the sum of the logarithms of its entries is log N. Choose a large fixed integer K and canonically pad each list to Kn bits. Use unary lengths Kn through 2Kn−1 for the individual bits. The intervals are disjoint across powers of two, and default to zero outside them. The bit language is tally, is in UP∩coUP, and has one advice bit per length. A P decider would reconstruct the entire list by O(n) queries of length O(n). Conversely a polynomial-time generator decides the tally language. The language is also in BQP via quantum factoring, so the conjecture would separate P from BQP as well as P from NP. [Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2004/160-2/p12) · [Shor](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9508027)

For a fixed integer C, enumerate signed sums D of at most C powers 2^e, 0≤e<n. For every current component R of N split it using gcd(D^(n+1) mod R,R), keeping only nontrivial splits. Powering to n+1 ensures that every prime power p^a dividing R is selected in its entirety exactly when p divides D. After all tests, two prime powers remain together exactly when their distinct primes have the same divisibility answer on every D. Thus the algorithm takes polynomial time for fixed C, including exact prime-power recognition of the components, and succeeds precisely when every pair of distinct prime factors is separated by some test. If one fixed C succeeds at all sufficiently large indices, finite exceptions can be hardwired and the hardness conjecture is false. Failure of this particular algorithm has no converse hardness implication.

It suffices to normalize the least exponent to zero and its sign to positive because N is odd. Repeated terms can be cancelled or combined, reducing the term count; exponents crossing n are wrapped with a sign since 2^n=−1 modulo N. In particular trivial cancellations yield no split. For every p dividing N the order of 2 modulo p is precisely 2n, since n is a power of two and 2^n=−1 mod p. All signed tests with at most two terms thus give identical divisibility for every p. A genuine split of F₅ first occurs at three terms: 641=1+2^7+2^9. For F₆ the smallest separating weight is five: 274177=1−2^8−2^12+2^14+2^18. The companion checker exhausts all 325,627 normalized tests of weight ≤4 for F₆. These are finite statements. No fixed bound at all sufficiently large indices has been proved. [Brent on Fermat number factoring](https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/brent/pd/rpb161.pdf)

Two deterministic controls further limit the conjecture. If at most one distinct prime divisor exceeds n^A for fixed A, bounded trial division and perfect-power recognition complete the factorization in polynomial time. A remaining odd semiprime R=pq also splits in polynomial time if |p−q|≤R^(1/4)n^A: the distance from √R to (p+q)/2 is (p−q)²/[2(√p+√q)²], so only polynomially many difference-of-squares trials are needed. If both factors are 1 mod M for an even M, test only midpoints congruent to (R+1)/2 mod M²/2. This follows directly from M² dividing (p−1)(q−1). Neither control guarantees a split at every index.
