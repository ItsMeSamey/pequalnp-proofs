# Contacts with an earlier triple before its surviving pin is deleted

An exact forward tour uses the [four physical reductions](degree-five-leaf-cap-pathwidth.md). At an earlier move, an added selected site X can change the row of a restored deleted site. A triple move has a second sensitivity: restoring it requires odd occupancy of C, which follows from the child q row only if that row has no unexpected selected neighbors. Thus an unexpected qX edge at an earlier triple may matter before q is deleted. This issue does not occur at a singleton triangle or paired move, whose inverse makes two fixed incidence changes at each surviving pin.

For a continuing tour on a degree-five host, materialize the recorded edge changes and remove the deleted vertices, on distinct clean labels down to the first sensitive step. Each nonpin still has ambient degree at most five. Indeed the only changed nonpins are C sites; each loses its actual incidence with u and gains at most one incidence with v. The only possible increase to a named pin is at its first designation as v in a triple. The other child pin q then has intended degree three. If that v also has intended degree at least three, this is a bounded high/high or exceptional terminal. In a continuing tour v therefore has intended degree one. A pin can survive only as q and can only retain or lose intended incidences; otherwise it is deleted. A pin cannot become an ordinary nonpin. It follows that the ambient degree of a high named pin at any further continuing move is also at most five. This invariant allows unrelated unused host sites to remain in the current graph.

In either triple shape, the removed nonpin u already has five intended neighbors, so an additional uX edge is impossible. In the adjacent triple shape q also already has five intended neighbors. This rules out an additional qX edge by the high-pin bound. In the nonadjacent shape q has degree three. If its qX edge is unexpected, the two edges pu and qX suffice whenever X misses p. Here uq and pq are absent, and uX is impossible. If X meets p as well, p,q,X and a clean private neighbor U of X form a claw. These repairs use literal sites and lift provided no earlier inverse is affected by their selected incidences. The appropriate earlier step is the first affected inverse, including a prior triple at q as just explained.

Every individual surviving pin has bounded lifetime in a continuing tour. Singleton triangles decrease its degree by two. The first triple can decrease degree five to three; thereafter triples retaining a degree-three q use the same fixed C=N(q). A long continuation forces the transferred pin v to be a leaf. Writing t=|N(v)∩C| before the move, this is possible only for t=2 or 3: its old degree is 2t−1 and at least t+1 because it has one further lost incidence. For t=2 the new leaf attaches to the one previously missing c in C. The next move is then a singleton triangle (the high pin q has degree three and is adjacent to that parent), reducing q to degree one. For t=3 the new leaf attaches outside C, and every c loses two incidences, one at u and one at v. No more than two consecutive such moves can occur, since each c started with at most five and retains qc. A remaining degree-one q is deleted at the next move, whether it is a paired leaf or the leaf at a mixed step. These bounds concern a continuing tour; a terminal has bounded order.

This note gives the degree and parity checks for the first affected triple. A full finite-window recognition theorem additionally needs a precise emission order, local degree checks, and a restoration argument for the other first-conflict types.
