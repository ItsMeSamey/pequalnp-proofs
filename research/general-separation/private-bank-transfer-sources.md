Williams's Theorem 1.5 relates faster Sum-Product algorithms to fixed-exponent lower bounds for exact linear combinations. His Lemma 3.1 checks an encoded circuit evaluator after fixing the circuit and gate index. For four private-bank atoms originally supplied on $N$ inputs, one can first branch on at most $4\gamma N$ private coordinates. When $4\gamma<1$, the remaining bounded-leaf trees admit a faster exact product count on those original inputs. A private set occupying even a small fixed fraction of the encoded evaluator can contain *all* coordinates left live by a particular hardwiring. The bank and direct-leaf budgets in the proofs here are charged to that evaluator's original arity. The parity-block construction and common choice of slots prove a smaller live footprint for every guessed atom before specializing gate indices. This supplies the restriction step needed to use the verification argument for this model. [Williams, Theorem 1.5 and proof of Lemma 3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.09121)

After this specialized evaluator has certified gap-UNSAT at the required exponential-size circuit scale, the argument applies Theorem 1.1 of Murray and Williams. Fixing the model exponents in advance gives a common polynomial size exponent for ordinary circuits under the contrary assumption. The resulting NP language and its infinite set of hard lengths can depend on those exponents. The [logarithmic-deficit expansion](private-bank-averaging-obstructions.md) identifies an input regime where the representation includes every ordinary circuit with the same gate budget.
