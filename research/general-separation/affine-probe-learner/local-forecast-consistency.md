# One, two, and three jointly feasible circuit forecasts

At width $n$ and a public numeric NAND gate cap $s$, each round presents an explicit circuit on the same hidden $n$-bit input. Constants and literal wires are allowed. For a labeled history $D$ let $V(D)$ consist of assignments satisfying **all** disclosed labels, including correct predictions. A deterministic predictor is $k$-wise feasible at a state with $V(D)\ne\varnothing$ if, for every list of at most $k$ permitted circuits, the forecasts on separate clones of that same state agree with those circuits at a single assignment in $V(D)$. The assignment may depend on the list. List probes are provisional. The initial history is both realizable and all-wrong (vacuously). An all-wrong history means every committed feedback bit has differed from its own preceding prediction, from initialization.

Consider two conventions. In the full-history convention feasibility is required at all realizable histories, with a uniform per-round time polynomial in $n+s+1$ and encoded history and query length. In the all-wrong convention feasibility is required only at realizable all-wrong histories, and the per-round clock must be polynomial in $n+s+1$ and the current query length, independently of horizon. In both conventions the predictor is total, including on inconsistent histories, with the applicable clock; a known polynomial timeout and a fixed timeout response suffice for the reductions.

Under either convention, existence of a polynomial-time one-wise feasible predictor for all widths and caps is equivalent to $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. No mistake bound is needed for the implication to SAT. The same equivalence holds for two-wise feasibility. Under $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$ a single two-wise learner meets the optimal $n$-mistake bound on every realizable stream, for each respective convention. Three-wise feasibility, even restricted to all-wrong states and with unlimited computation, allows an adversary to force $2^n-1$ mistakes at a linear cap. This number is tight: predicting by any one remaining assignment eliminates at least that assignment on each mistake.

## One forecast suffices after one mistaken prediction

With the current residual formula $G$, start the machine fresh and query $G$. If its forecast is one, some assignment satisfies $G$ by feasibility at the empty state, so accept. If the forecast is zero, give feedback one and ask for the next unfixed coordinate. Restrict $G$ by substituting the coordinate forecast for that variable, then restart. Whenever $G$ is satisfiable, the single-mistake history is realizable and the coordinate forecast preserves satisfiability. Whenever $G$ is unsatisfiable, every restriction remains unsatisfiable regardless of how the machine responds after the inconsistent feedback. An unsatisfiable residual therefore never causes acceptance at an empty state. After all coordinates have been fixed, evaluate the resulting constant. This takes at most $n$ restarts plus the final evaluation; for $n=0$ evaluate the input directly. Substitutions can keep unused inputs and the original gate cap, enlarged if needed for literal and constant queries. This yields a deterministic SAT decision procedure; ordinary decision self-reduction yields a full witness even if an early positive forecast ended the decision run. No unknown satisfying label is requested from an oracle.

Under the full-history convention a second, direct search conditions once on $F=1$. Forecast the coordinates successively and commit each forecast as its own label. On a YES input, one-wise feasibility guarantees that at least one model remains after each update. The resulting string must pass verification. On a NO input no string passes. This argument depends on feasibility after correct feedback, unlike the restart procedure.

## Two forecasts at a frozen state

Condition on $F=1$. On a YES input $V$ is the model set. Use the fixed numeric order and the comparators $T_j(x)=[x\le j]$, with constant sentinels $T_{-1}=0$ and $T_{2^n-1}=1$. With full-history two-wise feasibility the endpoints have the correct forecasts. Bisect using fresh clones of that one state and retain a zero-forecast left endpoint and a one-forecast right endpoint. At the resulting adjacent pair $T_{j-1},T_j$, a joint supporting model has to be $j$. Verify it directly. Reject if the sentinels or final verification fail. All comparators and $F$ fit a common cap $s=|F|+O(n+1)$ after basis conversion. Under the all-wrong convention, first forecast $F$ at the empty state. A positive forecast certifies YES immediately; a negative one makes the positive conditioning row a mistake and licenses the same frozen-state proof. As before, decision gives witness search via restrictions. If only individual feasibility is imposed at the frozen state, two models $u<v$ and a nonmodel $t$ between them allow a false threshold step $[j\ge t]$: each forecast has a supporting model but its rising adjacent pair has none.

## An exact $n$-mistake two-wise predictor under $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$

In the full-history convention keep the full history. In the all-wrong convention retain only mistaken rows. Let $V$ be the associated stored version space. It equals the genuine full space at every all-wrong history, and the full-history implementation has equality at every history. If empty, return a persistent default. Otherwise find its lexicographically least assignment $w$ by SAT calls on prefix restrictions.

For $S\subseteq\{0,1\}^d$ given by an efficient membership predicate, score the empty set zero. For nonempty $S$ put $r=d+1$. For $0\le k\le d$, ask whether there exist $r$ binary affine maps to $\{0,1\}^k$ whose images of $S$ together cover that target. Take $\sigma(S)=2^k$ for the largest passing $k$. The test has polynomially bounded quantifiers: choose the maps, universally choose a target, then exhibit an index and an input of $S$ mapping to the target. Thus it is in $\Sigma_3^P$, hence in P under the assumption. Nonemptiness and assignment recovery are also polynomial.

The score is monotone under set inclusion. For $M=|S|>0$ one has $M/2<\sigma(S)\le rM$. The upper bound counts possible images. For $2^k\le M$, the number of preimages in $S$ of a fixed target under a random affine map has mean $\mu=M/2^k\ge1$ and variance at most $\mu$ by pairwise independence. Cauchy–Schwarz gives probability at least $1/2$ of a preimage. For $r$ independent maps the probability some target is missed is at most $2^{k-r}<1$. Choosing $k=\lfloor\log_2 M\rfloor$ proves the lower bound.

Write $h=n+1$, $t=64h^2$, $d=nt$. For $A\subseteq V$ define $W(A)=\sigma(A^t)$ using the same $d$-bit domain for every query at that state. For a circuit $q$, forecast the label whose bin $A=\{x\in V:q(x)=a\}$ has the higher score. On a tie forecast $q(w)$ using the **same** $w$ for all queries at the state. Each product predicate checks the stored rows and the new circuit at $t$ explicit assignments.

Let $A,B$ be two chosen bins, with complements relative to $V$. If $A\cap B=\varnothing$, inclusion and the voting rule give

$$W(A)\le W(B^c)\le W(B)\le W(A^c)\le W(A).$$

Both votes would therefore be ties. Both chosen bins would contain $w$, a contradiction. Every individual forecast also has a witness because $V$ is nonempty and only the empty bin scores zero.

On a realizable mistake both bins are nonempty and their sizes satisfy $|A_{\rm true}|^t\le 2r|A_{\rm pred}|^t$. The surviving fraction is at most $(2r)^{1/t}/2$. Correct feedback cannot enlarge the stored space. After $h$ mistakes the initial mass $2^n$ would have fallen to at most $(2r)^{h/t}/2<1$: indeed $r=nt+1\le65h^3$ and $h\log_2(2r)/t\le11/64<1$. The hidden assignment survives, a contradiction. Independent fresh coordinate queries force $n$ mistakes by selecting each bit opposite its forecast. The mistake count is exact.

Both implementations may default permanently on a putative $(n+1)$st error, which keeps the mistaken-row store bounded even on inconsistent inputs. In the all-wrong convention all computations therefore use at most $n$ retained rows and the current query, giving the horizon-independent polynomial clock; it need not be feasible for the full history after an earlier correct response. The full-history implementation charges every stored row to the history-size clock.

## Three forecasts force singleton predictions

At any feasible all-wrong state read the forecasts of all $n$ coordinates on separate clones, forming a word $w$. Let $I_k$ test agreement with its first $k$ bits, for $0\le k\le n$. The constant $I_0$ must be forecast one. If $I_{k-1}$ is forecast one, joint feasibility for $I_{k-1}$, the $k$th coordinate, and $I_k$ forces $I_k$ to be forecast one: any common witness agrees with $w$ through bit $k$. Inductively the singleton test $I_n=[x=w]$ is forecast one, and its individual feasibility implies $w\in V(D)$. Each prefix test needs at most $O(n+1)$ NAND gates even after a long history.

While at least two assignments remain, present only the singleton test and report zero. This costs one mistake and removes exactly one assignment. The new committed history is again all-wrong and feasible. After $2^n-1$ such mistakes the sole survivor realizes the whole sequence. For any proposed polynomial bound $B(n,s)$, one can stop after $B(n,s)+1$ rounds at a linear cap once $2^n-1>B(n,s)$. Clone probes serve only to locate the query; determinism allows replay. No adversary label requires advance access to an unknown satisfying assignment.

The finite-class mistake argument builds on [Littlestone's halving algorithm](https://homepages.math.uic.edu/~lreyzin/papers/littlestone88.pdf). The monotone score refines the [statewise semantic construction](semantic-circuit-learning-equivalence.md) with a common witness for ties. These conclusions assume forecast feasibility; an ordinary small mistake bound alone supplies no such witnesses.
