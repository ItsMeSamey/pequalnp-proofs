# A single-history SAT reduction using positive forecasts on plain thresholds

An exact-n polynomial-time circuit learner with the following additional support property exists if and only if P=NP. The reduction below proves the forward implication. For the reverse implication, under P=NP the [two-wise feasible learner](local-forecast-consistency.md) supplies the property with a horizon-independent polynomial per-round clock in its all-wrong convention. An ordinary bounded learner need not satisfy the additional property.

All updates in a trial record an actual zero prediction and specify positive feedback. Other evaluations are provisional calls on independent copies of the identical state. An incremental learner runs in time polynomial per update in n and the public gate cap. A history-replay implementation may instead use polynomial time in the transcript supplied on each call. The reduction supplies at most n+1 committed rows in a trial. Both models require a defined response or an enforced polynomial timeout on inconsistent runs. Decision and witness guarantees are conditional on the selected mode of the supplied learner. Let a deterministic learner have an exact upper bound of n mistakes on every stream of circuits evaluated on a common n-bit assignment, at a cap accommodating the source, coordinates and ordinary integer thresholds. An assignment survives a history if it gives every label in it. On histories below whose first mistake is (F,1), assume that every positive forecast on a proper threshold T_j=[x≤j] has some survivor with T_j=1, whenever the history is realizable. The remaining mistaken labels in these histories are also one. No assumption is needed about negative forecasts or agreement across equivalent descriptions.

At initialization a positive forecast on F certifies satisfiability. Otherwise, if F were identically zero, feedback zero would be a genuine error preserving the entire cube. Ask the n distinct coordinates in turn and give each the opposite forecast. All responses are simultaneously realized by the word assembled from those bits, producing n+1 mistakes. This argument includes n=0.

If the initial forecast is zero, commit feedback one on F. For N=2^n define artificial forecast values zero at index −1 and one at N−1. On a YES instance the latter threshold always has a surviving positive assignment, provided the current history has a survivor. Bisect using the actual forecasts on indices 0,…,N−2 to find adjacent indices j−1,j with values zero and one; monotonicity is unnecessary. Check F(j) directly. If it holds, return j. Otherwise a surviving assignment at or below j must be strictly below j, by the positive-forecast hypothesis for an interior right endpoint or the induction hypothesis for the artificial right endpoint. Thus j=0 and F(0)=0 permit rejection immediately. For j>0 the forecast zero at j−1 was an actual learner forecast. Commit feedback one there. At least one witness survives all the accumulated labels, and the new round is a genuine mistake. No feedback is ever charged at an artificial endpoint. Reject after n+1 committed mistakes. On a YES input this would contradict the bound on a single surviving assignment. On a NO input the only affirmative exits have independently justified satisfiability.

For n=0 one can simply test F(0). In general each iteration consumes O(n+1) forecast calls with O(n+1) labels to replay and polynomial-time threshold construction. Fresh calls decide restrictions of F, giving witness recovery by ordinary bitwise self-reduction even when an affirmative exit was the first forecast. Fixing input wires to constants preserves the NAND gate cap up to the declared threshold overhead.

## A counterexample to charging faults across fresh restarts

Suppose instead the positive-forecast hypothesis holds only at the first state after (F,1). Consider a restart procedure which, after a false crossing U, replaces its source by F AND [x≤U−1] and restarts, retaining the order on integer assignments and the tightened upper sentinel. On the singleton at w=1 in dimension n≥2 it can lose exactly one candidate index at each restart.

To see this, begin with exact halving over all words. At initialization predict zero on a designated syntactic family of singleton sources: a fixed presentation of [x=1] and its nested conjunctions with recognized cutoffs at least one. These answers agree with initial exact halving. If the first mistake is a positive response on such a source there is only one genuine seed. At its immediate successor predict zero on every proper canonical threshold and predict one on the full threshold. On other queries predict their value at w. On the next mistake switch to evaluating every query at w for the rest of that run. This bounds the special runs by two genuine mistakes. Any positive threshold forecast at the special first state is sound. Elsewhere use usual mistake-only halving, whose positive forecasts are sound on realizable all-wrong histories. Although arbitrary-circuit halving may be inefficient, recognizing the special source/cutoff syntax and executing the special branch costs polynomial time. Given a hypothesized efficient baseline with the stated property one can also use this splice at initialization: ignore correctly answered special queries until the first feedback error, leaving the baseline in its prior state; when another query is forwarded, supply only actually forwarded rounds. Every forwarded subsequence stays realizable. In the mistake-only convention the initial empty-history baseline state is unchanged while such queries are skipped.

The special first state produces no positive interior forecast, so bisection returns the artificial upper endpoint U. Starting at U=N−1, the first success is at U=1, after N−2 false crossings. If the artificial upper endpoint is reset to N−1 on every restart, the same false crossing can recur. The witness is explicit in this example. It bounds the specified prefix-shrinking procedure and leaves open whether a different use of first-state soundness yields a general efficient SAT decision procedure.
