# Deterministic permutation inversion from online prediction on one circuit evaluator

Fix the evaluation rule \(E(q,s)=q(s)\), where the public query encodes a Boolean circuit and the private seed has \(n\) bits. Assume a uniform deterministic polynomial-time online predictor makes at most \(h=h(n)\) mistakes on every realizable stream of polynomial length with polynomial-size queries, for a known polynomially bounded, efficiently computable budget. An explicitly given polynomial upper bound suffices. Each prediction precedes disclosure of the corresponding bit. The predictor may be improper. All queries at a given length can be padded to the same size for a single universal evaluation circuit. The size bound must accommodate the iterates below.

**Theorem.** For any uniform polynomial-time length-preserving permutation family \(\pi_n\) of the Boolean cube, and any positive polynomially bounded integer horizon \(T\), there is a deterministic verifier-backed inverter whose failure fraction over uniform \(x\) on challenge \(y=\pi_n(x)\) is at most \(2nh/T\). The algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(n,T\) and the resources of the evaluation and prediction algorithms. Taking \(T=4n(h+1)\) gives failure below one half, and \(T=8n(h+1)\) gives failure below one quarter. Taking \(T\ge 2nh/\varepsilon\) gives failure at most \(\varepsilon\) whenever that horizon fits the promised resources. In particular, the predictor assumption contradicts the existence of a one-way permutation family in this model.

**Inverter.** Write \(\pi=\pi_n\). For each coordinate \(i\) use the ordered public queries \(Q_{i,\ell}(s)=(\pi^{T-\ell}(s))_i\), for \(1\le\ell\le T\), with \(\pi^0\) the identity. For every position \(j\), initialize a fresh copy of the same predictor. At each earlier position \(\ell<j\), obtain its prediction and then feed it the label \((\pi^{j-\ell-1}(y))_i\). Save the prediction at position \(j\). The estimate for bit \(i\) is the majority over all \(T\) saved predictions, with ties fixed to zero. Assemble the estimated string \(z\). Return it if \(\pi(z)=y\), and return failure otherwise. Injectivity certifies that a returned string equals the original input. The inverter performs at most \(nT(T+1)/2\) prediction steps. All prefix labels are available by forward iteration, and each query circuit can be generated uniformly by composing at most \(T-1\) copies of a forward circuit.

**Counting proof.** For fixed \(i,j,x\), the virtual seed \(s=\pi^{-(T-j)}(x)\) realizes the supplied prefix and has current label \(x_i\). Its inverse-power expression only indexes the proof. For each \(j\) the map from \(x\) to this seed is a bijection. Therefore, summing the simulated errors at position \(j\) over all challenges gives exactly the count of errors at position \(j\) across genuine full streams for all seeds. Summing over \(j\) gives at most \(2^nh\) errors for coordinate \(i\). Each incorrect majority requires at least \(T/2\) errors. At most \(2^n(2h/T)\) challenges have incorrect majority for that coordinate. A union bound across coordinates gives the claimed fraction. The guarantee is average-case over the original input. A particular challenge can belong to the exceptional set for every coordinate. Each fresh run receives a realizable prefix, and virtual seeds can differ between runs. The proof needs a mistake guarantee along a full stream of length \(T\), or an independently justified extension from the relevant short-game protocol.

**Relation to next-bit unpredictability.** The reverse order gives exactly the familiar ability to simulate an earlier-output prefix from a forward image in the Blum–Micali construction. Yao's next-bit framework relates next-bit prediction to tests for pseudorandomness. Here the premise is a uniform bound on the number of errors across the whole sequence for every seed. This permits counting errors separately for each original coordinate, enumerating every challenge position, and verifying the recovered full block. Individual coordinates need no hard-core assumption. These arguments give a conditional search reduction and provide no unconditional circuit or complexity-class separation. [Blum and Micali, *How to Generate Cryptographically Strong Sequences of Pseudorandom Bits*](https://doi.org/10.1137/0213053) · [Yao, *Theory and Application of Trapdoor Functions*](https://doi.org/10.1109/SFCS.1982.45)

**Invariant-distribution extension.** Let \(D\) be any probability measure on the seed cube invariant under \(\pi\). For each fixed \(j\), if \(x\sim D\) then \(\pi^{-(T-j)}(x)\sim D\). The sum over positions of the expected coordinate errors therefore equals the expected number of mistakes in the corresponding genuine stream with seed drawn from \(D\), at most \(h\). The same majority and union bounds give failure probability at most \(2nh/T\) under \(D\). The inverter uses neither samples from nor a description of \(D\). In particular the bound holds separately for the uniform law on each permutation cycle.

**Conditional RSA inversion.** Fix an ordinary valid RSA public key with modulus \(N=pq\) for distinct odd primes and a public exponent coprime to \(\lambda(N)\). Let \(n\) be the bit length of \(N\). On canonical encodings of the unit group \(G=(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times\), use the permutation \(u\mapsto u^e\bmod N\). Fix every other bit string. Range comparison and a gcd test recognize \(G\), so the extended forward circuit is uniformly constructible from the public key. The uniform measure on \(G\) is invariant. Consequently the hypothesized predictor gives, for each such key, a deterministic inverter succeeding on at least three quarters of uniformly distributed units when \(T=8n(h+1)\).

For every fixed unit challenge \(y=x^e\), a uniformly chosen unit \(r\) makes \(xr\) uniform on \(G\). Apply the conditional inverter at \(yr^e\bmod N\). On success its verified answer is \(xr\), so multiplying by \(r^{-1}\bmod N\) recovers \(x\). Independent trials have failure probability at most \(4^{-k}\) after \(k\) trials. Repeating until verification succeeds yields a randomized expected-polynomial-time inverter on every unit challenge for every valid key under the predictor assumption. Uniform units can be sampled in expected polynomial time by rejection and gcd testing. This is a conditional result about the RSA permutation on units. The reduction provides neither a factorization algorithm nor a predictor meeting its premise. [RSA blinding and unblinding operations, RFC 9474, §§4.2–4.4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9474.html#section-4.2)

**Consecutive failures on an orbit.** For an original input \(x\), call the position a failure if the deterministic inverter fails at \(\pi(x)\). Let \(X=\{x_0,\pi(x_0),\ldots,\pi^{a-1}(x_0)\}\) consist of \(a\) distinct positions on a single cycle of length \(L\), and let \(F_X\) count its failures. Then

\[
F_X T/2\;\le\; nh\min\{L,a+T-1\}.
\]

Indeed, all virtual seeds for these positions lie among the iterates of \(x_0\) with exponents from \(1-T\) through \(a-1\). For each coordinate and fixed virtual seed, a position \(j\) identifies at most one original input, since \(x=\pi^{T-j}(s)\). Its simulated error agrees with the error at that position of the seed's genuine full stream. Therefore all errors charged to one seed in one coordinate total at most \(h\), even when the interval wraps around the cycle. Each failure requires an incorrect majority in at least one coordinate and hence at least \(T/2\) errors. Combining the counts proves the inequality.

For an interval consisting entirely of failures and \(T>2nh\), this yields \(a\le 2nh(T-1)/(T-2nh)\). In particular \(a\le 4nh\) for \(T\ge 4nh\) and \(T>0\). The cyclewise bound already precludes a completely failing cycle in this range. Thus the failure positions cannot form a longer consecutive run. A success on a later forward iterate recovers that iterate's immediate predecessor, which can already be computed from the original challenge when the later iterate is strictly ahead. The interval bound alone gives no algorithm for stepping backward across a failing position.
