# Seven marked H8 edges cannot be owned by three products

Fix seven of the eleven edges of the graph H on 0,1,2,4,5,8,9,10, with edge list 09, 0–10, 14, 18, 25, 2–10, 48, 4–10, 58, 59, 89. Write U for those marks and T for the other four edges. H is C4-free of maximum degree four. A guarded support is empty, singleton, or a subset of the neighbors of an original G11 anchor to which all connecting edges are unmarked. Deleted anchors 3,6,7 see at most one H vertex, so a nonsingleton guard is contained in N_T(g) for an H vertex g. Let L be the union of these neighborhoods over g of T-degree at least two. There are at most two common exceptional supports E,F. Each is the union of two guarded terminals, except that the later F may be the union of E and one guarded terminal. Every product is a conjunction of positive disjunctions on guarded or exceptional supports, clean on H. Discard dead products, remove redundant conjuncts, and assign each covered U edge to exactly one product accepting it. This can be done even for repeated or overlapping products. Larger U reduces to seven by retaining any seven marks and their owners; their guards remain eligible. All statements about the number of edges or pivots in a batch below concern its owned marks.

**Matching bound.** No product can own a matching of three edges. Such a product must have an anchored nonsingleton clause: a singleton cannot meet disjoint marks, a single nonempty clause accepts a singleton, and two nonsingleton clauses either overlap on a singleton or force a K(2,2). For a cross edge ac of two matching marks ab,cd, a guard cannot contain a,d: its anchor and c both see a,d, forcing its anchor to be c and starring cd. It similarly cannot contain b,c. As it hits both marks its trace must be exactly ac or bd. A second cross of opposite parity contradicts this trace rule, and a cross bd creates a four-cycle. The nonedges ad and bc therefore must each be rejected by a distinct exceptional clause, since both hit every anchored clause; their traces on these marks are bc and ad, respectively. Each involved matching edge now has an E-only and F-only endpoint. Anchored clauses select opposite labels on two matching edges linked by a cross. If two crosses link i to j and j to k, every anchored clause selects the same label on i and k. Each of the two pairs joining opposite labels across i,k hits that clause and both exceptional clauses, so both pairs are edges, creating a four-cycle with marks i,k. Thus the third matching edge is isolated from any given conflict. Pick one of ad,bc and separately adjoin each endpoint of the isolated mark. Both triples are independent and meet every anchored clause and one exceptional clause. The other exceptional clause, which hits the isolated mark, cannot reject both triples. If instead no conflict occurs, eight independent endpoint orientations must each miss a distinct clause; at least six witnesses are guarded. Removing at most two orientations from the 3-cube leaves it connected and leaves both endpoint choices in every dimension. Adjacent guarded witnesses contain two common opposite endpoints, hence have the same anchor. That anchor would have six neighbors. This proves the matching bound, including sets with extra unowned graph edges.

**A double with at most one effective exceptional clause.** By the cross argument above two disjoint accepted marks must induce a matching. Rejection of the four independent endpoint pairs then needs at least three different complementary corner pairs contained in guards. Their anchors are outside all four marks, since an anchor equal to any marked endpoint would star its matching edge. With only four unmarked H edges available, three corners require one anchor seeing all four marks: an anchor on two marks supplies at most one corner, an anchor on three supplies at most two; incidences from distinct anchors to the marks are distinct. No deleted vertex can anchor a two-H-vertex corner. Only H vertex 8 has H-degree four, and the only induced matching on N_H(8) is 14,59. Thus up to disjoint ownership there is at most one double of this kind, and its four unmarked edges must be the four spokes at 8.

**Pivot accounting.** If h has at least two distinct owned mates, no guarded clause of its product can miss h; that guard would contain both mates, its anchor would have to be h and would star both marks. A clause missing h exists by singleton rejection. The intersection C of all missing supports contains every owned mate, and h is adjacent to every member of C since together they hit every clause. A guarded terminal contains at most one marked neighbor of any h, even without being contained in N(h): two would force its anchor h. If all of a terminal lies in N(h), a nonsingleton terminal contributes zero marked neighbors. A given C with at least two members fixes a unique center by C4-freeness.

If E,F are comparable, at most one effective exceptional clause occurs in any product. A pivot for the smaller support and one for the larger coincide if both exist, since both see the smaller and it has ≥2 members when used for a pivot. Across all groups there are at most three owned spokes at this center: all relevant mates are in the larger effective support, a union of at most three guarded terminals. If only one support supplies a pivot, the same estimate applies to it. Delete spokes at this center within the groups until matchings remain; at most two deletions suffice because ownership was disjoint. The three resulting matchings have total size at most four, since at most one may be the double 14,59. Thus the comparable case covers at most six.

If E,F are incomparable, they each have only two guarded terminals. A pivot missing only E sees all of E and two marked spokes force E to be precisely the two mates, both from singleton terminals. The same holds for F. A pivot missing both sees E∩F; it has at most two marked mates globally from that intersection because each E terminal supplies at most one. Thus each type (E only, F only, both) supports only one degree-two pivot across all disjoint ownership, and no pivot has degree three. All three types cannot occur: the two single-missing types would force E,F both to be distinct two-sets whose intersection has at most one member. There are at most two pivots in all.

A row with three or more edges contains a pivot by the matching bound. If one of its pivots misses only E, write E={a,b}, its two mates at center h. Every edge of that row hits E. A third owned edge makes a or b a second pivot, say a. Since a belongs to E, it misses only F, so F={h,r}. Four or more edges would require both a,b to be pivots (each has maximum degree two), which together with h gives three. Thus the row owns exactly three, and the other rows must be matchings. If E,F are disjoint, all graph edges hitting both form a 2-by-2 bipartite corner set with at most three edges, all owned already. If they overlap then r=b and every edge hitting both either contains b or is the already owned ah. Thus no further owned matching of size two can use both E,F. To reach seven there would have to be two such matchings using at most one effective exception, contradicting uniqueness of the homogeneous double. The F-only case is symmetric.

It remains that a row W with at least three edges has a pivot h missing both E,F, with mates a,b in their intersection. Incomparability and these common mates force both exceptions to have at least three vertices. Thus neither single-missing pivot type can occur, and h is the only owned pivot anywhere. Discard one edge at h to leave three matchings, each of size at most two. To have seven owned edges, all three must be doubles after this deletion: W originally owns three, the other rows two each. This also excludes the pattern 3+3+1. W's third edge cd is disjoint from h,a,b; neither a nor b can be a pivot since each belongs to both exceptions. Every one of the three products must have a nonsingleton guarded clause: singleton clauses cannot meet a disjoint double, and without guards the common singleton a would be accepted. In W that guard contains h and an endpoint of cd. Thus h∈L, and every marked edge in any group hits L. Also at least one of a,b lies in L, since if both were outside L each would require its own singleton terminal in E (and F), leaving no possibility of incomparable supersets. There is therefore a marked edge inside L incident to h.

**Four unmarked edges.** Every edge of H outside L must be unmarked. Its vertex-cover number is at least five: without 8 an independent set uses at most two vertices of the cycle 0–9–5–2–10–0 and at most one of the edge 1–4; with 8 its remaining candidates are 0,2,10, which also have independence number two. A connected acyclic T component of four edges can only be a star, path or fork (degree sequence 3,2,1,1,1); any cycle is triangular, as H has no four-cycle.

* Four disjoint unmarked edges give L empty. A two-edge path plus two isolated edges has |L|=2 and L together with one endpoint per isolated edge covers H, impossible.
* Two disjoint two-edge paths, a four-spoke star, or a triangle with a pendant edge give |L|≤4 with all four unmarked edges meeting L, contradicting the same cover lower bound. A three-spoke star or a triangle plus one isolated edge has |L|≤3, and adjoining one endpoint of the isolated edge again gives a cover of size at most four. These exhaust the disconnected component-size partitions except a three-edge path plus an isolated edge.
* For a three-edge path plus isolated edge, L consists of the four path vertices. At least ten H edges meet L (all seven marked plus the path). At least three are internal. The degree sum of L is at most 13: only 8 has degree four and all other H vertices have degree at most three. Therefore exactly ten meet L and exactly the three unmarked path edges are internal. This contradicts the marked internal edge ha (interchange a,b if needed). Explicitly the only possible induced paths at equality are 5–8–4–10 with isolated 09, or 9–8–4–10 with isolated 25: the three remaining path vertices have H-degree three in {4,5,9,10}.
* For a connected five-vertex path or fork, L consists of all five component vertices. The remaining three vertices O are independent because no unmarked edge leaves the component and every mark meets L. Each has H-degree at least two. Thus H[L] has at most five edges, four unmarked and at most one marked. There is exactly one marked internal edge, and exactly one of h's mates lies in L (call it a); the other mate b is in O. All three members of O then have degree two, so O={0,1,2}, L={4,5,8,9,10}. The four unmarked edges are the tail 8–4–10 and any two of the triangle 5–8–9 edges.

In the last case none of the three matching rows can omit an effective exception because the four unmarked edges are not the four spokes at 8. Hence every mark hits both E and F. Each of E,F contains b outside L, necessarily as a singleton terminal; its other terminal must contain a and at least one additional vertex (the exception has size at least three). That other terminal is a nonsingleton guard entirely in L. Pick any o∈O distinct from b. It belongs to neither exception, and all its H edges are marked. Each of its at least two neighbors therefore lies in E∩F. But h also neighbors all of E∩F, contradicting C4-freeness for h and o.

This exhausts the allowed shapes and proves F(2,3)≤6 without enumeration. The independent 330-mask numerical check, when available, remains a distinct verification route.
