# F(2,1) ≤ 3 on H8

Let H be G11 induced on 0,1,2,4,5,8,9,10. An ordinary guard is a singleton or a subset of one original star neighborhood; anchor-to-member edges are absent from the assigned residual set. A clean product is a conjunction of such guards and possibly exceptional clauses E,F. Each binary exception is the union of two guards, except that the later exception can instead join the first to another guard. We use the one-binary-exception lemma in singleton_6648_q2_sole_or_all_c_cases.md: at most two residuals per clean product, disjoint double marks form an induced matching, and touching doubles force the exceptional clause to consist exactly of the two mates on the surviving domain. Its proof applies on induced H with original anchors (C4-free, original degree ≤4); the inability to accept a matching of three does not require a bound on the exceptional child count.

If only one exceptional clause remains effective it is a union of at most three guards. Three disjoint marks are impossible. If p has two marked neighbors, every ordinary clause contains p; the exception then excludes p and lies inside N_H(p) by pair activation. Another pivot would force this exception, which contains both p-mates, into the intersection of two distinct neighborhoods, impossible. Thus four marks require at least three p-spokes (otherwise a 2-star and two isolated edges give a matching of three). Each residual mate must be supplied by a distinct child: a guard containing two neighbors of p must be anchored at p and would star its marked mate. Indeed a contributing child lies in N(p) and can contain no other H vertex. Three spokes exhaust the three children, so the exception consists exactly of the mates; no further separate marked edge hits it. Four spokes require four children. This covers nested and comparable effective exceptions. With no exceptions the bound is stronger.

Otherwise E,F are incomparable effective factors, each a binary union of ordinary children. If these are the only clauses, singleton rejection forces disjoint supports and their full cross product is a biclique, hence a star; its singleton side reduces to an ordinary guard. Take a common ordinary clause G. Suppose four distinct marks. Splitting E into the two alternative child branches gives two clean products each with the single exception F; each accepts ≤2. Therefore the two marks sets are disjoint pairs covering the four. The symmetric F split also partitions 2+2.

Every pivot p of marked degree ≥2 belongs to every common ordinary clause: otherwise it contains two mates of p and its only possible anchor p stars their edges. At least one exceptional clause omits p by singleton rejection. Its two ordinary children hit at most one p-neighbor each, so the marked graph has maximum degree two. In particular three consecutive distinct pivots cannot occur: all belong to G and any star neighborhood in a C4-free graph induces a matching. A singleton G also cannot contain three vertices. A matching of four is impossible. G would need at least four vertices and its anchor cannot be any marked endpoint: that endpoint's own marked edge must hit G at its mate, hence be starred. All eight H vertices would be marked, while the outside vertices 3,6,7 have respectively zero, one, one H-neighbors.

Choose a pivot p with mates u,v. If E contains p, one E child owns both spokes. The touching-double lemma for that branch forces F={u,v}. All four marks meet this two-set, forcing u and v both to have degree two and no marked uv. They give three consecutive pivots u,p,v. Thus both E and F omit every pivot. An edge joining two pivots could not hit either exception. Two distinct pivots p,q cannot be nonadjacent in the marked graph either: both exceptions must contain all their mates, while all ordinary clauses contain both pivots. Pair activation makes p and q adjacent to each of the two distinct mates of p, contradicting C4-freeness. Hence the marks are two p-spokes and two disjoint off-spoke edges e,f.

Each E child owns precisely one p-spoke, and hence one off edge. Its double is an induced matching. Thus p misses all four off endpoints; each p-mate misses both endpoints of at least one off edge. G must cover at least three disjoint marks, so its anchor s has at least three H-neighbors. It cannot be a marked endpoint by the own-edge argument and cannot be outside H, so it is the unique unused H vertex. Since G contains p and hits e,f, s neighbors p and touches both off edges. Therefore N_H(p) consists exactly of its two mates and s, and e,f form a perfect matching outside N_H[p].

The degree-three H vertices are 4,5,9,10. For 4 the unique outside matching is 09,25 on 0,2,5,9; both 8 and 10 are neighbors of 4 touching both edges, and at least one is a mate, which is impossible. For 5 the unique outside matching is 0–10 and 1–4; neighbors 2 and 9 touch only the first edge, 8 only the second. For 9 the unique outside matching is 1–4 and 2–10; neighbors 0 and 5 touch only the second, 8 only the first. For 10 the unique outside matching is 1–8 and 5–9; neighbors 0 and 2 touch only the second, 4 only the first. None of these last three pivots has an eligible s. This exhausts four marks.
