# First-fork coloring for signed circuits

Consider a fan-in-at-most-two AND/OR/NOT DAG with donor wires as sources. The observable is a scalar output or a fixed read-once AND/OR/NOT formula on t labeled port occurrences. Each port occurs exactly once and there are no other varying inputs in the aggregation. Repeated taps of the same wire count separately. The instance below uses OR. Each pin occurrence and each output tap is a use. Trace only valid, semantically read pins: NOT reads its first pin; missing and forward reads are constant false. F is the sum of uses beyond the first over all live producers, including the donors themselves. A donor without any uses is isolated in every conflict graph below.

## Coloring lemma

Starting at each live donor (including that producer itself), follow its unique sequence of forward uses until a producer with at least two uses is encountered. Color it by that first fork. If its route ends at an output without a fork, assign the common no-fork color. For multiple ports attach the fixed aggregation formula to the output taps. Each old output tap is replaced by one corresponding leaf use; the read-once aggregation creates no forks. Missing ports contribute constants. Unused donors may take an arbitrary color.

Take distinct live equal-colored donors x,y that are jointly essential in at least one section. Their paths first meet at a vertex m at or before their common fork, or in their path to the final scalar observable if neither encounters a fork. Neither donor itself can be this merge: donor sources have no incoming edges. Before m each donor has a unique path, and no side input on either path can depend on either donor. Otherwise an earlier fork or merge would exist. Therefore on any background fixing every other donor on which both x and y matter, each path transmits its input with a polarity determined just by its NOT gates. AND and OR with a fixed side input either transmit unchanged or block entirely. At m a binary AND or OR combines one literal from each path. All subsequent influence of x and y passes through m. Fixing the other donors leaves the downstream computation a unary function of m, even if m fans out and reconverges. In a section where both inputs matter this function is identity or complement.

Hence an equal-colored pair has one fixed minority corner on every two-essential section of the scalar observable. A minority corner is the unique corner whose output differs from the other three. Its location depends on m's operation and the two fixed literal polarities. Complementing the whole table changes the minority value but leaves the location unchanged.

## Two conflict certificates

1. A fully forced positive AND square and positive OR square at different backgrounds require minority corners 11 and 00. Such a pair must have different colors. Each square needs four direct, or otherwise logically forced, scalar values without any assumption of monotonicity. On the revised 15-donor bank, 33 pairs have at least one fully forced positive AND square, two pairs have at least one fully forced positive OR square, and none have both.
2. A separate conditional certificate needs three directly forced values per background. Fix a center and the two adjacent corners of a square. If the center differs from each adjacent corner, the two donor bits are both essential on this section under every completion. Under the equal-color hypothesis the section has a unique minority corner. It must be the center: any other singleton would leave at least one of its two neighbors equal to it. Thus two observed wedges with distinct center locations reject the equal-color hypothesis. This proof does not assert a fourth target value or extend the promise by monotone closure. The two backgrounds can even coincide if their evidence is consistent and rules out any one-minority table.

In either conflict graph the coloring is proper. If chi is its chromatic number, chi ≤ number of fork vertices + 1 ≤ F+1. Let g count all charged gates, t output taps and s distinct certified necessary donors. With h traced gates and r traced donors, the number of valid incidences is h+r+F ≤ 2h+t. Using h≤g and r≥s proves g+t−s ≥ F ≥ chi−1. More generally this bound applies to any conflict edge supported by observations excluding every common minority-corner choice for two-essential sections. A chromatic lower bound requires a certificate such as an explicit clique.

## Window application

For AND roots 213, 222, and 225 of the 181-gate control, observe their OR. On a promised cell where the unchanged branches already cover the target positive, the replacement OR is unconstrained. On every other cell it must equal the target. All 15 donors in the revised bank were independently certified necessary. The [wedge certificates](wedges.json) give 14 conflicting pairs; donors 65,102,107 form a triangle, and the supplied three-coloring is proper. The certificates include concrete row/column pairs, full donor signatures and forced values. They use only direct observations.

At least two distinct live fork producers are necessary at every gate budget. A single producer with three uses gives F=2 yet cannot suffice. Consequently F ≥ 2. Even allowing NOT gates, any three-port replacement over this bank needs at least 14 gates, since g+3−15 ≥ 2. A one-port replacement over this bank needs at least 16 gates. The 14/16 gate counts recover the existing bounds. The stronger two-fork conclusion holds at every gate budget. These bounds apply to the specified cut, donor bank and untampered context. They do not establish a lower bound for the whole circuit under other rewrites or other donors.

An external arbitrary decoder is outside the theorem. Raw ports x,y,c use no forks; a free decoder selecting x AND y or x OR y according to c yields a conflict. Likewise a partial table completed by assuming monotonicity does not certify the fully forced square in case 1 for a circuit with unchecked NOT gates.

Even a fixed XOR decoder falls outside the aggregation basis: XOR(x,y) on two distinct raw taps gives opposite directly witnessed wedge centers with zero forks. For monotone closure, observed f(10)=f(01)=1 and f(00)=0 admit an XOR completion with f(11)=0. Upward closure would incorrectly assert f(11)=1 unless monotonicity is independently established.
